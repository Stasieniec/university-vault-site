# PhilSci-L06: Scientific Realism and the Pessimistic Meta-Induction

> [!abstract] In short
> Laudan's pessimistic meta-induction: the aether, phlogiston and caloric do not exist, yet the theories built on them were successful, so success is no sign of truth and our best theories are probably false too. Can a realist still believe what science says about the unobservable?
>
> **Selective realists:** yes, if you are selective. Kitcher says old terms like "aether" referred on some occasions of use; Psillos says believe only the parts of a theory that produced its success. **Stanford:** those parts can only be picked out with hindsight, so the method cannot say which parts of today's theories to believe: a Pyrrhic victory, one that costs as much as a defeat.
>
> **De Haro's extensional realism:** sort no parts, and separate two kinds of meaning, what a word describes (its intension) and what it picks out (its extension). Descriptions can change completely while what is picked out, in the domain where a theory was tested, stays the same. So "aether" referred all along: in that domain, to the electromagnetic field taken in a fixed frame.
>
> No mock exam question is on this lecture (the mock's realism question is in [[PhilSci-L05 - Scientific Realism and its Critiques|L05]]). The last part is the research of Sebastian De Haro, who coordinates the course and sets the exam. The exam questions below are practice questions.

## Where the debate stood after week 5

**Scientific realism** says our best theories are approximately true, so the unobservable things they posit (electrons, fields) exist. The lecturer, Enrico Cinti, gave each argument from [[PhilSci-L05 - Scientific Realism and its Critiques|L05]] a verdict.

**For realism, each with some force:** inference to the best explanation (van Fraassen: there is no such rule, and the rival hypothesis, that we only infer the best explanation to be empirically adequate, fits our practice as well); the no-miracles argument (success, above all novel prediction, would be a miracle if theories were not approximately true); the demand for explanation; and Grover Maxwell's argument that the theory-observation distinction cannot be sharply drawn.

**Against realism:**

- **Empirical under-determination** (the same evidence fits rival theories): probably a **stalemate**, with **not much force**. Equivalent rivals are easy as toy models, rare in real physics. Realists say **theoretical virtues** (simplicity, beauty, fruitfulness) break the tie, being **truth-conducive**; anti-realists deny it, and neither side can prove its case.
- **Constructive empiricism** (van Fraassen: science aims only at empirical adequacy): the **main contender**. It has fewer of the problems of logical empiricism and some virtues of realism, but where "observable" ends **remains a problem**.
- **The pessimistic meta-induction**: **some force**.

## The challenge, restated

Reminders from L05:

- **The pessimistic meta-induction (PMI, Laudan 1981):** many once-successful theories posited things we now think do not exist, so by an induction over theories (hence "meta"), ours will probably turn out false too.
- **Laudan's list:** crystalline spheres, humoral medicine, phlogiston, caloric, the optical and electromagnetic aether and more, "in every case a theory which was once successful and well confirmed, but which contained central terms which (we now believe) were non-referring". The **aether** was a medium thought to fill all space, carrying light and electromagnetic waves as air carries sound. **Phlogiston** was a substance thought to be released in burning. **Caloric** was heat as an invisible fluid flowing from hot to cold.
- **Convergent realism**, Laudan's target: successive mature theories are approximately true, keep the reference of their terms, and explain their predecessors' success.
- **Saatsi's two readings** (Juha Saatsi): the PMI predicts that current theories are false, or, more modestly, shows that success is not a reliable sign of truth.

Laudan's three problems for the realist:

1. There is no good notion of **approximate truth**.
2. There is no connection between **empirical success** and approximate truth, so the chain success, approximate truth, reference breaks.
3. **Truth requires reference** (a claim can only be true of what its words pick out), but old theories contain **obviously non-referring** terms.

Keep an eye on "obviously": De Haro denies it.

### What realists actually claim

Kitcher and Psillos first correct the target. Van Fraassen defined realism as: "Science aims to give us, in its theories, a **literally true** story of what the world is like; and acceptance of a scientific theory involves the belief that it is true." Realists in fact believe theories **approximately or probably true**. And a theory is **not monolithic**: it has parts (laws, mechanisms, posits), some more important than others. So "approximately true" can mean the important parts are true even if others are false, which is how they aim to make Laudan's first problem precise.

## First reply: only mature, genuinely successful theories count

Psillos first requires success to include **novel predictions**, since any theory can be made to fit known facts. Then maturity (Boyd; Hardin and Rosenberg): be a realist only about **mature** theories. A discipline is mature once past a **take-off point**, where entrenched background beliefs fix the domain and constrain new theories (for heat: no perpetual motion, heat flows from warm to cold, Newtonian mechanics). Humoral medicine and effluvial electricity drop off the list.

**Kyle Stanford** (2006), the main critic here: the maturity requirement is **arbitrary and post hoc** (made up after the fact), and some listed theories surely were well confirmed: **phlogiston theory of combustion**, **caloric theory of heat**. Psillos had already conceded in 1999 that caloric theory and the optical aether theories were mature, successful and false. Maturity shrinks the list without defeating the PMI.

## Second reply: believe only what did the work

**Selective confirmation:** success confirms some parts of a theory and not others. Kitcher selects among uses of a term; Psillos among parts of a theory.

### Kitcher: reference is fixed case by case

Philip Kitcher: scientists' terms **do not automatically refer**; look at each context. "Aether" might refer to the **electromagnetic field** on some occasions, to **empty space** on others, to **nothing at all** on others. So Laudan is wrong that such terms simply fail to refer. Reference attaches to each **token** (one occasion of use), fixed by the speaker's intention then. Kitcher's example: the chemist Joseph Priestley called oxygen "dephlogisticated air" (air with its phlogiston removed), and some of his uses of the phrase refer to oxygen.

**Stanford's reply:** this misses the point of the meta-induction. Even if central terms refer, they sit inside **theories that repeatedly turn out radically misguided**, which cannot be approximately true. It is "little comfort to the realist" if those tokens referred, "while admitting that the relevant theoretical accounts and descriptions of those entities were mistaken about virtually everything except the fact that the entities in question played some causal role in producing observable phenomena". Realism needs approximate truth; reference is only a precondition.

### Psillos: divide et impera

*Divide et impera* is Latin for "divide and rule".

> [!definition] Psillos' selective confirmation
> - **Causal core:** the parts that explain the phenomena and are **responsible for the theory's success**. Typically **retained** by later theories.
> - **Idle constituents:** parts **not causally involved** in explaining the phenomena. Typically **disappear** later.

Success confirms only the core: the assumptions that "really fuel the derivation" of a success, which it cannot do without and no non-ad-hoc alternative could replace.

> [!example] Maxwell's theory and the aether
> The standard case is James Clerk Maxwell's electromagnetism (the nineteenth-century theory uniting electricity, magnetism and light; a different Maxwell from L05's Grover Maxwell). Its causal core is its **equations and mechanisms of explanation**, which do not require the aether. The aether as a material medium is an **idle constituent**, not retained. So the success confirms the equations, and dropping the aether shows nothing against them.

Psillos' own case, the nineteenth-century optical aether, is subtler. What was dropped was mainly the mechanical **models** of the aether (an elastic solid), which scientists treated as heuristic devices; the general dynamics of the light-carrying medium, including energy conservation, carried over into electromagnetism. And Psillos argues that "luminiferous ether" plausibly **refers to the electromagnetic field**.

**Compare Lakatos** ([[PhilSci-L01b - Popper and Lakatos|L01b]]): both split a theory into a part that carries the weight and a part that can go, but Lakatos' **hard core** is what scientists decide to protect, while Psillos' causal core is defined by its role in producing success.

### Which theory of reference?

- **Descriptive:** a term refers to whatever fits the theory's description. When descriptions change, reference changes, so the central terms on Laudan's list fail to refer. The PMI quietly assumes this.
- **Causal** (Kripke, Putnam): a term refers to whatever caused the phenomena that led scientists to introduce it. "Aether" then refers to the electromagnetic field, which plays the causal role of carrying the energy. Complaint: reference becomes **too easy**; almost any abandoned term refers to something.
- **Mixed** (Papineau, Lewis, Psillos): a term refers to an entity that **both causes the phenomena and satisfies a causal description** of how it does so. The causal contact anchors the description; the description stops reference coming too cheaply. This is Psillos' route to "ether" referring to the field.

## Third reply: keep only the structure

**Structural realism** (John Worrall, 1989) splits a theoretical claim into its **content** (what the entities are), which gets superseded, and its **mathematical structure**, which is retained. (Standard example, from outside the readings: Augustin Fresnel's equations for light survived into Maxwell's theory; Fresnel's elastic aether did not.)

**Objection:** structure may **not be enough to specify an ontology**: what kinds of things the world contains, which was the point of realism. Psillos' core keeps laws and mechanisms with their content. De Haro also wants more than structure: matching equations (formal correspondence) are not enough for him, and his extensions include entities with their properties.

## Stanford: a Pyrrhic victory

Back to Psillos' causal core. Stanford's **historicist** critique (testing philosophy of science against the actual history) has four points.

1. **The core/idle split is anachronistic.** The scientists would have denied it: **Maxwell regarded the aether as essential**. How can we say his theory "was really not about the aether"?
2. **Convergence is virtually guaranteed.** Almost by definition, whatever current theories retain is called the "causal core", so its survival is built in.
3. **We need prospectively applicable criteria.** A core found by looking back at what survived works only on past theories. For ours we would need to know which parts will survive, which is what we do not know. Without such criteria, selective realism is **Whig history** (the past written as a march towards the present), and we are not entitled to realism about our best theories.
4. **The dilemma.** Either the core/idle split matches what past scientists believed was doing the work, which needs facts about their beliefs we do not have; or it does not, and they repeatedly misidentified what produced their success.

> [!definition] Pyrrhic victory
> A victory so costly it is no better than defeat. Selective realism beats the historical record only by sorting past theories with hindsight. If past scientists repeatedly misjudged which parts of their theories did the work, today's probably misjudge ours too. So the method says nothing about which parts of our own theories to believe, and that was the point of realism.

**Psillos' answer:** scientists draw the line themselves, at the time. Lavoisier, Laplace and Carnot believed the caloric laws that fuelled their predictions while treating "heat is a material fluid" as speculative. Stanford's aether case targets exactly this: Maxwell did not treat the aether as idle.

**Stanford's later paper**, "'Atoms exist' is probably true, and other facts that should not comfort scientific realists", moves away from reference. Grant that "atom" refers: beliefs about atoms still changed profoundly. The real question is whether science will keep showing a "broad pattern of repeated, profound, and unpredictable changes in fundamental theoretical orthodoxy". And the trouble with an abandoned term is that it is no longer part of "the most pragmatically powerful and successful conceptual apparatus" for its phenomena.

His 2006 book also raises the **problem of unconceived alternatives** (background): past scientists kept missing equally good rivals later adopted, so ours probably have some too.

## De Haro's extensional scientific realism

### What De Haro takes from Stanford, and what he rejects

De Haro grants that Stanford's critique of selective confirmation "seems generally on the mark", including that past scientists counted beliefs such as the aether as core parts of their theories. Two lessons stand:

1. **Explain the connection between reference and approximate truth**, with an account of both.
2. **Do not project current insights into the past**; read past scientists' arguments **in their historical context**.

Two claims are rejected:

- **Convergence is not guaranteed.** Every term of an old theory could have turned out idle, leaving **none of the original terms**. That some survive is **highly remarkable**, a real finding about history.
- **Stanford reads more into Maxwell's text than is there**: that Maxwell found energy travelling without an aether "incoherent" and "unintelligible". The text does not show this.

So De Haro accepts Stanford's demands and rejects his claim that successive theories lack real continuity.

### The diagnosis: one word, two kinds of meaning

The literature from Putnam (who stated an early PMI in 1978) to Stanford assumes **a single kind of meaning**. Hence Laudan's perplexity: there are plausible arguments both that "aether" refers (Kitcher, causal theories) and that it does not (descriptive theories). When both sides look right, a distinction is missing, and philosophy of language (Gottlob Frege, a founder of modern logic; Carnap; Lewis) has it.

> [!example] The morning star and the evening star
> Venus is seen both in the morning and in the evening, and was called both "morning star" and "evening star". The names **mean different things**, "the brightest star in the morning" and "the brightest star in the evening", yet in our world both pick out **one object, the planet Venus**.

> [!definition] Sense and reference (intension and extension)
> - **Sense = intension:** the **linguistic meaning** of the words. *"The brightest star in the morning"; "the brightest star in the evening".*
> - **Reference = extension:** the **actual object or entity** the words refer to. *Venus.*
>
> "Sense" and "reference" are Frege's terms; "intension" and "extension" are **Carnap's**.

Why the two come apart: a description says what would count as the morning star in any situation, so an intension reaches every situation the words could describe. The extension is what actually fits the description in one situation, our world, under our conditions of observation. That both descriptions pick out one object is a discovery about our world. Two terms are **extensionally equivalent** when they pick out the same thing in the situations in question, even if their intensions differ. For a theory, De Haro narrows those situations to where it has been well tested: its **domain of application**.

### Temperature: different intensions, one extension

- **Thermodynamics:** temperature is "a measure of the tendency of an object to give energy to its surroundings".
- **Statistical mechanics** (heat as molecular motion): "the mean kinetic energy of the molecules in a substance".

Different definitions, so **different intensions**; the same physical quantity, so **one extension**. This defuses Kuhn's claim that scientists in different paradigms "live in different worlds" ([[PhilSci-L02 - Kuhn on Scientific Practice|L02]]): the **intension** changed, the **extension** did not. The two physicists give different accounts of what temperature *is*, and still measure the same quantity.

### The answer: believe extensions, within the domain

The PMI denies **continuity of reference and approximate truth** between discarded theories and successors. Two steps:

1. **Continuity of extensions.** Successive theories are **extensionally equivalent**: they refer to the same items in their domain of application. Example: everything classical mechanics says about its domain can be derived from quantum mechanics. Psillos got continuity from retained parts; De Haro gets it by **delimiting a theory's extension to its domain of application**.
2. **Approximate truth in terms of extensions** (last subsection).

> [!definition] The slogan of extensional scientific realism
> *We are justified in being scientific realists about extensions but not necessarily about intensions.*

**Why stop at extensions?** An intension applies wherever the words could be used, including situations where the theory was never tested and may be false. The evidence supports only the extension within the domain, and prudent scientists never claim more. So a successor that differs radically **outside** the old domain is no threat: nobody had reason to believe the old theory there. De Haro's example: for navigation and the motions of the sun and moon, at ordinary accuracy, Newton's "gravitational force" and general relativity's spacetime curvature cause the same phenomena (Newton's force can be derived from general relativity), so there they are extensionally equivalent.

This also meets Stanford's criterion of the most powerful conceptual apparatus. In its domain, Newton's gravity is still the most powerful apparatus we have, and so is Maxwell's theory in its domain. And reference does matter: dismissing it treats an extension as a bare, "faceless" object, but extensions are partly fixed by intensions, so they carry content.

> [!definition] What determines an extension
> The extension is determined by the **intension** together with the **specific circumstances or context** in which a phenomenon is studied.

As "morning star" and "evening star" coincide only given how our world is and how we observe it, scientific terms depend on:

- the **model** specified (initial and boundary conditions, environment);
- the **values of free parameters** (mass, coupling strength, concentration);
- **extra-theoretical facts** (experimental error, other influences, weather);
- **approximations and idealisations**: $v \ll c$ (speeds far below light speed $c$, where relativity reduces to Newton), $\hbar \to 0$ (Planck's constant to zero, quantum effects vanish), $G \to 0$ (gravitational constant to zero, gravity off).

So the domain is no fudge: scientists already state these conditions. And an intension can be wrong in general yet, in the right circumstances, pick out the right thing. This makes De Haro's account a mixed theory of reference, like Psillos': descriptions (intensions) and practical contact (experiments, samples, measurements) together fix reference.

### How to show two theories share an extension

> [!definition] Three kinds of correspondence
> 1. **Predictive (numerical and formal) correspondence:** the two theories make the **same predictions under the relevant approximations**: the same values (numerical), laws of the same form (formal).
> 2. **Material correspondence** (including experimental and instrumental correspondence): **the system studied is the same**, seen from different points of view.
> 3. **Conceptual correspondence:** the concepts are **not identical but "match"**: they play the same roles in the given extension (the situations and things both theories are applied to).

- **Predictive:** quantum mechanics reproduces classical mechanics under specific conditions (large systems, among others):
$$\left\langle \frac{dp}{dt} \right\rangle = -\langle \nabla V \rangle \quad\longrightarrow\quad F = -\nabla V$$
where $p$ is momentum, $V$ potential energy, $\nabla V$ its gradient, $\langle \cdot \rangle$ the quantum average over the state, $F$ the classical force. Since $F = dp/dt$, the quantum law for averages has the form of Newton's second law.
- **Material:** the **atom** studied by the quantum theorist and the inorganic chemist is the same entity, though their experiments differ (particle accelerator, electron microscope, chemical reaction).
- **Conceptual:** "position of a particle" is a sharp point in classical mechanics and a spread-out quantity in quantum mechanics. But both give the system's location, both stand in the same relation to momentum, both are measured the same way, and the quantum average obeys Newton's law. In classical mechanics' domain they do the same job.

In general all three are needed: two descriptions of one sample that ascribed conflicting measurable properties would not be equivalent, whatever the material match.

### The aether exists

The pessimist: **"aether" does not refer**, so how can the old theory be even approximately true? Stanford calls its ontology "radically misguided". Old and new theories are in predictive and material correspondence, but conceptual correspondence seems to fail: a material medium filling space has no counterpart in modern theory.

De Haro: **"aether" does refer. The aether does exist.**

> [!definition] What "aether" refers to
> What Maxwell called the "aether" is **extensionally equivalent** to what we now call **"the electromagnetic field", together with a fixed frame of reference**, for speeds far below light ($v \ll c$). "Aether" and "electromagnetic field" are, of course, **intensionally distinct**.

(A frame of reference: a standpoint for measuring positions and speeds.) De Haro thinks **Maxwell's argument for the aether was very good**: electromagnetic energy does not vanish at the sender and pop up at the receiver elsewhere (**conservation of energy**), so something in between carries it. That "medium" is the field together with a fixed frame. On a natural reading, the missing conceptual correspondence is then there: in the domain, Maxwell's medium and our field play the same role, carrying the energy. Why a fixed frame? A plausible reason (background physics, not De Haro's argument): a medium is at rest in one frame; the field singles out none.

**How this differs from Psillos.** Psillos also lets "aether" refer to the field. The difference is the route: Psillos sorts the theory into core and idle parts and anchors reference in what did the work, the sorting Stanford attacks. De Haro sorts nothing: he takes Maxwell's argument at face value, treats the aether as real, and says only that what the argument established is, extensionally, the field.

**Phlogiston and caloric.** Of the terms on Laudan's list, only the aether is worked through. For caloric, De Haro cites John Norton's example of extensional equivalence: "caloric" refers to the same thing as the "heat" of thermodynamics, within situations with no interchange of heat and work. Cases like "dephlogisticated air" he calls controversial (realists either deny the theory was mature or argue the term sometimes referred) and, either way, not really a problem for realism.

### No parts, so no Pyrrhic victory

Stanford's dilemma came from sorting theories into parts. **Extensional realism sorts no parts.** It is a linguistic and philosophical theory of **how to interpret scientific statements**, treating all terms alike: for all we know (assuming empirically adequate theories and sound arguments), **all the terms that scientists claim refer, do refer**. It needs no facts about hidden intentions and never says scientists misidentified their successes. Hindsight finds a domain's borders, but reference needs none: Newton's "gravitational force" already referred where he had applied his theory.

**The parallel with van Fraassen:** he interprets a theory **literally** but believes only what it says about the **observable**. Extensional realism takes literally what the scientist says, but **believes it only in its extensional, not intensional, meaning**.

> [!warning] Extensional realism is still realism
> The shape is shared (literal reading, restricted belief); the restriction differs. Van Fraassen's line falls between observable and unobservable, so electrons are out. De Haro's falls between extension and intension, and electrons, forces and fields are extensions, so they are in. The observable/unobservable line plays no role in it.

### Approximate truth as truth on a larger domain

The last step addresses Laudan's first problem. A theory is **extensionally true** on a domain when what it says, taken extensionally, is true of the situations in that domain; it is approximately true when extensionally true on its domain of application. To compare theories, De Haro borrows **Laudan's own measure of scientific progress**: the relative number and significance of the problems a theory solves. Read a domain as a set of problems solved: a theory right in more, and more important, situations has made more progress.

> [!definition] Closer to the truth (De Haro's sketch)
> $T$ is closer to the truth than $T'$ **iff** $T$ is **extensionally true in a larger, and more significant, domain of application**.

**Worry:** could a theory cover more by saying less? The standard answer: $T$ must also be **as good a theory as $T'$**, for example in predictive power and explanation.

> [!example] Comparing two theories
> An old theory is extensionally true on domains $D_1$ and $D_2$. Its successor is true on larger domains $D_1' \supset D_1$ and $D_2' \supset D_2$, and on a further domain $D_3$. The successor is closer to the truth, and the old theory is **not refuted**: it was, and still is, extensionally true on $D_1$ and $D_2$, so approximately true.

**The upshot.** Stanford is partly right, but his "broad pattern(s) of repeated, profound, and unpredictable changes in fundamental theoretical orthodoxy" misses the **restricted domains of application** where successive theories are extensionally equivalent: intensions can change radically, extensions change piecemeal. Two of Laudan's problems get a direct answer: approximate truth has a (sketched) definition, and the aether refers. The link between success and truth De Haro mostly sets aside: he assumes a theory well confirmed in its domain, and says belief in its extensions is then justified.

## Are we in the same position as past scientists?

The PMI assumes our situation is like that of past scientists.

- **Laudan**, with the philosopher Mary Hesse's "principle of no privilege": yes, our theories are as liable to radical conceptual change.
- **Psillos:** not quite. Scientists learn from failures and improve their methods, and many posits survived several revolutions: gene, atom, chemical bond, electromagnetic field. This works only against Saatsi's first reading; the second needs no analogy, since past successful but false theories already show success is no reliable sign of truth.
- **Stanford:** yes; future scientists will judge ours with hindsight.
- **De Haro:** past scientists were justified only within their domains, and there their beliefs mostly survived. What changed were intensions and claims about untested domains.

>
> - **Kitcher (1993):** terms do **not automatically refer**; look at the specific context. Reference is **fixed differently in different cases**: "aether" could refer to the electromagnetic field in some cases, to empty space in others, and to nothing in others. So Laudan is wrong that such terms are simply non-referring.
> - The mechanism: reference is assigned to individual uses (tokens), fixed by the speaker's intention, so some of Priestley's uses of "dephlogisticated air" refer (to oxygen) and others not.
> - **Stanford (2006):** this misses the point of the meta-induction. Even if central terms refer, they sit in **theories that repeatedly turn out radically misguided**, which cannot be approximately true.
> - Stanford's words: little comfort if the accounts of those entities "were mistaken about virtually everything except the fact that the entities in question played some causal role in producing observable phenomena".
> - Point: realism needs approximate truth; reference is only a precondition.

> [!exam]- Practice question: How does extensional scientific realism avoid Stanford's "Pyrrhic victory" objection, and how is it parallel to and different from van Fraassen's constructive empiricism?
> **Key points:** Dilemma comes from sorting theory parts; extensional realism sorts no parts, for all we know all claimed terms refer; differs from Psillos in route, not in the aether's referent; parallel: literal reading, restricted belief; difference: extensions include unobservables.
>
> - **Stanford's objection** targets selective confirmation: sorting theories into parts needs past scientists' intentions or says they misidentified what made their theories succeed, and works only with hindsight.
> - **Extensional realism distinguishes no theory parts.** It is a linguistic/philosophical theory of how to interpret scientific statements: for all we know (assuming empirically adequate theories and sound arguments), **all terms scientists claim refer, do refer**.
> - So it never declares the aether idle: it takes Maxwell's argument at face value and says what it established is, extensionally, the field. Psillos also lets "aether" refer to the field, but by sorting parts; De Haro's route needs no sorting. Hindsight only finds a domain's borders; reference needs none.
> - **Parallel:** van Fraassen reads theories literally but believes only what they say about the observable; De Haro takes literally what the scientist says but believes it only in its **extensional**, not intensional, meaning.
> - **Difference:** extensions include unobservables (fields, electrons, forces), so extensional realism is a realism; the observable/unobservable line plays no role.

> [!exam]- Practice question: Is Laudan right that our current epistemic predicament is analogous to that of past scientists? Discuss with reference to the replies to the pessimistic meta-induction.
> **Key points:** The PMI needs the analogy (principle of no privilege); Psillos: better methods and many surviving posits; Saatsi's second reading needs no analogy; Stanford: future scientists will judge us with hindsight; De Haro: justified belief was restricted to domains, and there it survived.
>
> - **Why it matters:** the PMI infers our fate from past scientists' fate, so it needs our situation to be relevantly like theirs (Hesse's "principle of no privilege").
> - **Against the analogy (Psillos):** scientists learn from failures and improve methods, so newer beliefs are better supported; many posits survived several revolutions (gene, atom, chemical bond, electromagnetic field).
> - **But (Saatsi):** that only answers the first reading of the PMI. On the second, past successful but false theories already show success is no reliable sign of truth, whatever our position.
> - **For the analogy (Stanford):** realists pick out what survives only with hindsight, and future scientists will do the same to our theories.
> - **Reframing (De Haro):** past scientists' justified beliefs, within their domains, mostly survived (Newtonian gravity, Maxwell's field); what changed were intensions and claims about untested domains.
> - A good answer takes a side, for example: the analogy holds for intensions and fails for extensions.

## Flashcards

> [!card]- How does the realist claim to break empirical under-determination?
> With **theoretical virtues** (simplicity, beauty, fruitfulness): non-empirical but truth-conducive.

> [!card]- What was the aether supposed to be?
> A **medium filling all space** that carried light and electromagnetic waves, as air carries sound.

> [!card]- What are Laudan's three problems for realism?
> No good notion of **approximate truth**; no link from success to approximate truth; truth needs **reference**, yet old terms seem obviously non-referring.

> [!card]- What do Kitcher and Psillos say realists believe about successful theories, against van Fraassen's definition of realism?
> Their **approximate or probable truth**, not their literal truth.

> [!card]- How does treating theories as having "parts" help with Laudan's approximate-truth problem?
> "Approximately true" can mean the **important parts are true** even if others are false.

> [!card]- How does Psillos tighten "success" before restricting Laudan's list?
> Success must include **novel predictions**, since any theory can be made to fit known facts.

> [!card]- What is the "mature theories only" reply to the pessimistic meta-induction (Boyd, Hardin and Rosenberg)?
> Be a realist only about **mature, well-confirmed** theories; the immature ones on Laudan's list do not count.

> [!card]- What is Stanford's objection to the "mature theories only" reply?
> Maturity is **arbitrary** and post hoc, and some listed theories were well confirmed: **phlogiston** and **caloric**.

> [!card]- How does Kitcher reply to Laudan's claim that terms like "aether" were non-referring?
> Terms do not automatically refer: **reference is fixed differently in different contexts**.

> [!card]- On Kitcher's account, what three things could "aether" refer to in different cases?
> The **electromagnetic field**, **empty space**, or **nothing at all**.

> [!card]- What fixes the reference of a token of a term, on Kitcher's account?
> The **speaker's intention** on that occasion of use.

> [!card]- What is Stanford's objection to Kitcher's account of the reference of old theoretical terms?
> Even if the terms refer, the theories were **radically misguided**, so cannot be approximately true.

> [!card]- In Psillos' divide et impera, what is the causal core of a theory?
> The part that **explains the phenomena** and is **responsible for the theory's success**; typically retained later.

> [!card]- What does Psillos call the parts of a theory that play no role in its success and typically disappear later?
> **Idle constituents**.

> [!card]- How is Psillos' divide et impera applied to Maxwell's electromagnetic theory?
> The equations and mechanisms of explanation are the **causal core** and need no aether; the **aether is idle**.

> [!card]- What is the difference between Psillos' causal core and Lakatos' hard core?
> Lakatos' hard core is what scientists **decide to protect**; Psillos' causal core is defined by its **role in producing success**.

> [!card]- Why does a purely descriptive theory of reference favour Laudan's pessimistic meta-induction?
> A term refers to whatever fits the description, so when **descriptions change, reference changes**.

> [!card]- What is the complaint against purely causal theories of reference?
> They make reference **too easy**: almost any abandoned term ends up referring to something.

> [!card]- On Psillos' mixed theory of reference, when does a term refer to an entity?
> When the entity both **causes the phenomena** and **satisfies a causal description** of how it causes them.

> [!card]- What does Worrall's structural realism say is retained across theory change, and what is lost?
> Retained: the **mathematical structure**. Lost: the **content**, what the posited entities are.

> [!card]- What is the main objection to structural realism as a reply to the pessimistic meta-induction?
> Preserved structure may not be enough to specify an **ontology**: what kinds of things the world contains.

> [!card]- What is Stanford's anachronism objection to Psillos' selective confirmation?
> The core/idle split would have been **denied by the scientists themselves**: Maxwell regarded the aether as **essential**.

> [!card]- Why does Stanford say convergence on the causal core is "virtually guaranteed" in selective realism?
> **Almost by definition**, whatever current theories retain counts as the causal core.

> [!card]- Why does Stanford demand "prospectively applicable" criteria of idleness?
> Hindsight sorting works only on past theories; it cannot tell which parts of **our current theories** to believe.

> [!card]- What is Stanford's dilemma for selective confirmation?
> Either know the **beliefs and intentions of past scientists**, or say they **repeatedly misidentified** what produced their success.

> [!card]- Why does Stanford call selective realism's win over the pessimistic meta-induction a "Pyrrhic victory"?
> It rescues past theories only **with hindsight**, so it cannot say which parts of **current theories** to believe.

> [!card]- How does Psillos answer the objection that the core/idle line is drawn only with hindsight?
> Scientists drew it **at the time**: Lavoisier, Laplace and Carnot believed the caloric laws but doubted heat was a fluid.

> [!card]- According to Stanford's "'Atoms exist' is probably true", what is the real trouble with an abandoned term?
> Not failed reference: it is no longer part of the **most pragmatically powerful conceptual apparatus** for its phenomena.

> [!card]- Which two lessons does De Haro accept from Stanford's critique of selective confirmation?
> Explain **reference and approximate truth** together, and **do not project current insights into the past**.

> [!card]- Why does De Haro deny that convergence on a causal core is "virtually guaranteed"?
> Every term could have proved idle, leaving **none of the originals**, so a surviving core is highly remarkable.

> [!card]- What weakness does De Haro find in the realism literature from Putnam to Stanford?
> It assumes **a single kind of meaning** of words, which produces Laudan's perplexity about reference.

> [!card]- What is the intension (Frege's sense) of a word?
> Its **linguistic meaning**.

> [!card]- What is the extension (Frege's reference) of a word?
> The **actual object or entity** the word refers to.

> [!card]- What does the "morning star" and "evening star" example illustrate?
> **Two different senses, one reference**: both names pick out Venus.

> [!card]- Why can two terms with different intensions share an extension?
> An intension covers **every situation**; the extension is what fits it in **one situation**, and there they can coincide.

> [!card]- On De Haro's account, what do the thermodynamic and statistical-mechanical uses of "temperature" share?
> The same **extension** (one physical quantity), despite different **intensions**.

> [!card]- How does the intension/extension distinction reinterpret Kuhn's claim that scientists in different paradigms "live in different worlds"?
> The **intension** changes between theories, the **extension** need not: both still refer to the same thing.

> [!card]- What are the two steps of De Haro's answer to the pessimistic meta-induction?
> 1. Continuity of **extensions** (extensional equivalence). 2. Define **approximate truth** in terms of extensions.

> [!card]- What is extensional equivalence between two theories, for De Haro?
> Reference to the **same items in the domain of application**.

> [!card]- How does De Haro's extensional equivalence secure continuity differently from Psillos' selective confirmation?
> Psillos: via **retained theory parts**. De Haro: by **delimiting the extension to a domain of application**.

> [!card]- What is the slogan of De Haro's extensional scientific realism?
> We are justified in being realists about **extensions** but not necessarily about **intensions**.

> [!card]- Why should a realist commit only to a theory's extension in its domain of application, according to De Haro?
> The intension covers **untested situations** where the theory may be false; evidence supports only the tested extension.

> [!card]- What determines the extension of a term in a scientific theory, according to De Haro?
> The **intension** plus the **specific circumstances or context** in which the phenomenon is studied.

> [!card]- What three kinds of correspondence establish extensional equivalence, for De Haro?
> **Predictive** (numerical and formal), **material**, **conceptual**; in general all three are needed.

> [!card]- What is conceptual correspondence between two theories, for De Haro?
> The concepts are **not identical** but **match**: they play the same roles in the given extension.

> [!card]- The quantum theorist and the inorganic chemist study the same atom with different experiments. Which kind of De Haro correspondence is this?
> **Material** correspondence.

> [!card]- On the pessimist's account of aether theory, which kind of correspondence with modern electromagnetism is missing?
> **Conceptual**: a material medium seems to have no modern counterpart.

> [!card]- According to De Haro, what is Maxwell's "aether" extensionally equivalent to?
> The **electromagnetic field together with a fixed frame of reference**, at speeds far below light ($v \ll c$).

> [!card]- What was Maxwell's argument for the existence of the aether, which De Haro calls very good?
> **Conservation of energy**: energy cannot vanish at the sender and reappear at the receiver, so something carries it.

> [!card]- Psillos and De Haro both let "aether" refer to the electromagnetic field. What is the difference?
> The route: Psillos **sorts core from idle parts**; De Haro **sorts nothing**, treating all terms alike within a domain.

> [!card]- How does De Haro's extensional realism escape Stanford's "Pyrrhic victory" dilemma?
> It **distinguishes no theory parts**: for all we know, all terms scientists claim refer do refer.

> [!card]- What do De Haro's extensional realism and van Fraassen's constructive empiricism have in common?
> Both **read theories literally** but **restrict belief**: van Fraassen to the observable, De Haro to extensions.

> [!card]- Why is De Haro's extensional realism still a realism, unlike constructive empiricism?
> Extensions include **unobservables** (fields, electrons); the observable/unobservable line plays no role.

> [!card]- What is the difference between Kitcher's and De Haro's ways of saving the reference of old terms like "aether"?
> Kitcher saves it **token by token**; De Haro saves it **for all terms alike**, within the domain.

> [!card]- In De Haro's sketch, when is theory $T$ closer to the truth than $T'$?
> Iff $T$ is **extensionally true in a larger, and more significant, domain of application**.

> [!card]- On De Haro's account, is an old theory refuted when a successor is extensionally true on a larger domain?
> **No**: it remains extensionally true, so approximately true, on its own domain.

> [!card]- What does De Haro say Stanford's "repeated, profound, and unpredictable changes" in theory miss?
> **Restricted domains of application** where successive theories are **extensionally equivalent**.

> [!card]- What is Mary Hesse's "principle of no privilege"?
> Our theories are **as liable to radical conceptual change** as past theories were.

> [!card]- Why does Psillos deny that our position is just like that of past scientists?
> Scientists **learn and improve methods**, and many posits (gene, atom, electromagnetic field) **survived** several revolutions.

> [!card]- Why does "we are better placed than past scientists" not answer Saatsi's second reading of the pessimistic meta-induction?
> That reading needs no analogy: past **successful but false** theories already show success is no reliable sign of truth.
