AGAI-Lab02: Learning Agents

In short

Agents now learn from their own rewards by trial and error, each independently, and the lab asks whether such learners reach a good equilibrium and whether a more powerful learner helps. In the Stag Hunt, two independent Q-learners mostly lock into the safe (D,D): initial values decide the equilibrium, and since one agent’s exploration is noise in the other’s rewards, exploration knocks them out of (C,C) but almost never out of (D,D). In the Coin Game, a grid-world social dilemma, tabular Q-learners learn to collect coins but never to leave the other’s alone, so their total reward stays near that of random agents. Irrelevant inputs blow up the Q-table and ruin learning; a neural-network learner (PPO) shrugs them off but still defects. Function approximation fixes generalisation, and the conflict of incentives remains.

The lab is ungraded practice that the lecturer calls the best preparation for the midterm. The notebook ships without outputs, so simulation numbers come from my own re-implementation of its models with the lab’s parameters (own random numbers, own PPO code); they show the expected behaviour but can differ in detail from a classroom run. Analytic results are exact.

Concepts tested: Q-Learning, Independent Learning, Epsilon-Greedy Policy, Exploration vs Exploitation, Stag Hunt, Prisoner’s Dilemma, Nash Equilibrium, Social Dilemma, Tabular RL, Function Approximation, PPO, Actor-Critic, REINFORCE

Part I: independent Q-learning in the Stag Hunt

The model

The game is Lab 1’s Stag Hunt with R=4 (both hunt stag), S=0 (I hunt stag alone), T=3 (I hunt hare while the other hunts stag) and P=2 (both hunt hare). C is stag, D is hare. It has two pure Nash equilibria, (C,C) and (D,D), and Lab 1’s threshold belief is

p∗=(R−T)+(P−S)P−S​=1+22​=32​:

stag is the best response only if the partner hunts stag with probability above 2/3. Two Lab 1 benchmarks come back: rational agents with uniformly random beliefs reached (C,C) in 11% of encounters, and under replicator dynamics a population went to all-stag only from starting shares above 2/3, so from one third of the starting states.

The learners. The stage game repeats unchanged and nothing is remembered, so there is a single state. Each agent i keeps two numbers, Qi​(C) and Qi​(D), its estimates of what each action earns, and uses stateless Q-learning:

Qi​(ai​)←Qi​(ai​)+α(ri​−Qi​(ai​))

where:

  • ai​ is the action agent i just played, and only its value is updated
  • ri​ is the payoff it just received
  • α is the learning rate, the fraction of the prediction error ri​−Qi​(ai​) that is corrected (0.1 throughout)

Regular Q-Learning learns Q(s,a) per state and adds a look-ahead term to the target, r+γmaxa′​Q(s′,a′), which credits an action for the state it leads to (γ is the Discount Factor). With one state, maxa′​Q(s′,a′) is the same number whichever action was played, so it shifts both targets equally and never changes which action looks better. Dropping it (equivalently γ=0) leaves the rule above.

Actions are [[Epsilon-Greedy Policy|ε-greedy]]: with probability ε a uniformly random action, otherwise the action with the larger Q-value, ties broken at random. So an agent whose greedy action is C plays D with probability ε/2.

Each agent learns from its own action and reward only and treats the partner as part of the environment: Independent Learning (Lecture 3, AGAI-L03 - Intro to Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning). Because the partner is learning too, the payoff of each action keeps changing: a moving target.

What is measured. Each experiment runs 500 independent pairs for 3000 rounds. The outcome of a run is its greedy joint action, what the two would play now without exploration: (C,C), (D,D) or miscoordinated. An agent counts as a stag hunter only if Q(C)>Q(D) strictly, so a tie counts as hare.

Q1: predictions before the runs

Statement

(a) Two independent Q-learners play the Stag Hunt (R=4,S=0,T=3,P=2) 3000 times in a row. They start knowing nothing (all value estimates equal to 0), and choose a random action (explore) 10% of the time. The experiment is repeated 500 times. In what fraction of the runs do you expect them to end at (C,C)? Choose one: less than 10%, 10-40%, 40-70%, more than 70%.

(b) Now suppose both agents start at (C,C): they already hunt stag and believe that this pays off. Will they keep hunting stag if they explore 10% of the time? And if they explore 50% of the time?

(a) Agents that know nothing start by playing at random, which is like holding the belief p=1/2<p∗. Against a coin-flipping partner D earns more than C (2.5 against 2, Q2(b)), and the replicator benchmark puts x=1/2 in the basin of all-hare. So (C,C) should be rare: less than 10%. My runs give 5% to 10% across seeds (mean 6.6% over ten).

(b) A rational agent would stay: against a partner who explores 10% of the time, C earns 3.8 and D 2.95 (Q4). The learners mostly do not: at ε=0.1 about 80% of runs leave (C,C) within 3000 rounds, at ε=0.5 almost all within a few dozen rounds.

(a) At round 3000 the Stag Hunt learners end at (C,C) in 5% to 10% of runs (across seeds) and at (D,D) in the rest; the Prisoner’s Dilemma learners end at (D,D) in every run. Learning did not help: the learners do worse than Lab 1’s rational agents (11%) and far worse than the replicator dynamics (one third). In round 1 about 28% of runs settle at (C,C) and the rest at (D,D); after that exploration slowly knocks (C,C) runs over to (D,D) (Q4), and none come back.

(b) In the first rounds all Q-values are 0, so both agents break ties at random: the partner is a coin flip. Against it

E[r∣C]=21​⋅4+21​⋅0=2,E[r∣D]=21​⋅3+21​⋅2=2.5.

D earns more on average, and, more important for a learner, never less than 2, while C earns 0 half the time. Round 1 in detail:

  • Both played C: both earned 4, Q(C)=0.4>0, so both now prefer C. Probability 1/4.
  • Both played D: both earned 2, Q(D)=0.2>0, so both prefer D.
  • One of each: the D player earned 3 and prefers D. The C player earned 0, so it is still tied; as soon as its tie-break picks D it earns 2 and prefers D.

So a run reaches (C,C) essentially only if both play C in round 1, probability 1/4 (28% in my run; a few mixed runs also meet at C in round 2 when the D player explores). Lock-in is quick because of a feedback loop: once my partner plays D, C pays me 0, so I never see evidence that C could pay 4, and my D in turn makes C pay 0 for my partner. Q(C) can only rise when both play C in the same round, which during exploration happens in (ε/2)2=0.25% of rounds.

(c) No. In the Prisoner’s Dilemma D is dominant (T=5>R=3 and P=1>S=0), so (D,D) is the unique Nash equilibrium. There was no other equilibrium to coordinate on: the learners found the only one, and their values reflect it (final average Q(D)≈1.2=0.95⋅1+0.05⋅5). The bad outcome is a Social Dilemma, an incentive problem: (C,C) Pareto dominates (D,D), yet each agent does better by defecting whatever the other does. In the Stag Hunt (C,C) is itself an equilibrium, which the learners could have reached and mostly missed: that is a coordination failure.

(a) (C,C) survives at ε=0.01, mostly survives at 0.05, is mostly lost at 0.1 and entirely at 0.5, although C stays the best response in every row.

Unrolling the update shows what Q(C) is:

Qt​(C)=α∑k≥0​(1−α)krt−k​(plus a vanishing term from the initial value),

where rt−k​ is the payoff of the k-th most recent C play: an exponentially weighted average of the recent payoffs of C. The weights 0.1,0.09,0.081,… halve about every 6.6 plays, so Q(C) remembers roughly the last 1/α=10 payoffs. With a constant learning rate it estimates “what C paid lately”, a noisy quantity, and never settles on the expected value 3.8.

At ε=0.1 the partner plays D in 5% of rounds, and each such round pays me 0 and cuts my Q(C) by 10%. From 3.8, three zeros give 3.42→3.08→2.77, below Q(D)≈2.95. Three zeros within a short stretch are enough, and over 3000 rounds, with each agent exposed to the other’s exploration, such a streak is likely. For a payoff with variance σ2 the weighted average has standard deviation σα/(2−α)​; with σ2=16⋅0.05⋅0.95=0.76 that is 0.20 around 3.80 (my runs: mean 3.80, standard deviation 0.20, minimum about 2.0).

Once one agent’s Q(C) dips below its Q(D), it plays D, and the partner earns 0 every round and follows within about three rounds. Both may retry stag a few times while their stale Q(C) is above 2, as the optimist did in Q3(b), and some pairs re-coordinate, but each failed retry costs another 10% until (D,D) holds. At ε=0.5 C leads by only 0.25 and a single zero (3.0→2.7<2.75) is enough, so (C,C) falls within a dozen rounds.

So the learners leave a Nash equilibrium because one agent’s exploration is noise in the other’s rewards, and a constant learning rate makes each agent react to that noise as if the partner had changed.

(b) At (D,D) with ε=0.1, C pays 0.05⋅4=0.2 on average and D pays 0.05⋅3+0.95⋅2=2.05. For C to become greedy, Q(C) must climb from about 0.2 to above 2:

  • Q(C) is updated only when I explore C (5% of rounds), and pays 4 only if the partner explores C in the same round (probability 0.05). The other 95% of my C plays pay 0 and pull it back down.
  • Each success moves Q(C) only 10% of the way to 4: 0.2→0.58→0.92→1.23→1.51→1.76→1.98→2.18, about seven successes with no failure in between.
  • Even then both must switch together: one that switches alone earns 0 against a hare hunter and switches back.

Starting at (D,D) (both pessimistic) with ε=0.1, my runs saw one transition to (C,C) in 500 runs of 3000 rounds, and it fell back. Structurally these are the Stag Hunt’s basins: leaving (C,C) needs the partner to look like a defector more than 1−p∗=1/3 of the time, leaving (D,D) needs it to look like a cooperator more than p∗=2/3 of the time. In Lab 1’s terms (D,D) is risk-dominant (P−S=2>R−T=1), and noisy learners drift to it and stay.

Common pitfalls in Part I

  • “C is the best response, so learners stay at (C,C).” A constant-rate learner tracks recent payoffs and reads a streak of exploratory defections as a change in the partner.
  • “More exploration helps coordination.” In the Stag Hunt it destroys (C,C) and leaves (D,D) intact.
  • Calling the Prisoner’s Dilemma result a coordination failure. It has one equilibrium, and the learners found it.

Part II: the Coin Game with tabular Q-learning

The model

The Coin Game (Lerer and Peysakhovich, 2017) turns the repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma into a grid world. Two agents, red and blue, move on a 3×3 grid that always holds exactly one coin, red or blue.

  • The grid is a torus (walking off one edge enters the opposite one), so every cell is at most two moves away.
  • Actions: up, down, left, right. Both agents move at the same time and may share a cell.
  • An agent picks up the coin by moving onto its cell; both can take the same coin in the same step. A new coin of random colour then appears on a random cell not occupied by an agent.
  • An episode lasts 16 steps; then agents and coin are placed at random again.
  • Each agent observes four one-hot 3×3 grids (36 numbers): its own position, the other’s position, the coin if it has its own colour, the coin if it has the other’s colour.

A pickup pays the picker +1 whatever the coin’s colour, and taking the other player’s coin costs that player 2:

pickuppickercoin’s ownertotal
own coin+1(the picker)+1
other’s coin+1−2−1

Going for your own coins and leaving the other’s alone is the cooperative pattern; taking every reachable coin is defection. Both are patterns of movement the agents must discover from positions.

Three measures, averaged over the games of an update: individual performance (coin pickups per episode, both agents together, any colour; random agents get about 3.7), restraint (the share of pickups of the picker’s own colour: 0.5 for colour-blind agents, 1 for full restraint) and welfare (total reward per episode, red plus blue). From the table,

welfare=pickups×(2×restraint−1),

so welfare needs both skill and restraint, and colour-blind experts score about 0.

The learners. Each agent runs regular Q-Learning with a table (Tabular RL):

Q(s,a)←Q(s,a)+α(r+γmaxa′​Q(s′,a′)−Q(s,a))

where s is the state read from the observation (own cell, other’s cell, coin cell, whether the coin is mine: 9⋅9⋅9⋅2=1458 rows), a one of the four moves and s′ the next state, with α=0.1, γ=0.95, ε=0.1 and all values starting at 0. The γmax term now matters: it credits a move for bringing the agent closer to a coin. Each agent treats the other as part of the environment. Training runs 32 games in parallel; one update is one 16-step episode in each, so 512 decisions per agent.

The lab’s text speaks of 300 updates, but the training function’s default, which its cells run, is 1000. I report values at update 300 and at the end of the 1000-update run.

Q5: predictions before the runs

Statement

(a) Two independent Q-learners are trained with their own rewards. After training, what share of each agent’s pickups do you expect to be coins of its own colour?

(b) How large will the total reward per episode (red + blue) be, compared with two agents that move at random?

(a) About half. Nothing in an agent’s own reward pays for restraint (Q6(b)), so a self-interested learner takes whichever coin it can reach, and colours are random.

(b) About the same as random agents, close to 0. The learners will pick up many more coins, but colour-blind pickups add nothing to the total on average (welfare formula). Both predictions hold (Q6).

(a) Individual performance rises steadily from the random level to about 17 coins per episode for the pair after 1000 updates (4.6 times random), and is still creeping up. Restraint stays near 0.5: a small bump to about 0.6 around updates 100 to 200 fades back to 0.52. Welfare follows the formula, 16.9×(2⋅0.52−1)≈0.7, so the total ends barely above random agents’ (about 0) with more than four times the pickups. The agents got good at the individual task and no better at the social one.

The early bump is not cooperation. A likely reason, read off the payoffs: taking a coin rather than letting the other take it is worth 1−(−2)=3 to me for my own coin and 1−0=1 for the other’s. So a selfish learner races harder for its own coins, and once both reach every coin fast, that preference matters less.

(b) Compare taking a coin with leaving it, from my own reward only. Taking it pays +1 now, whatever its colour. Leaving the other’s coin pays me 0, and if the other then takes it I still get 0. The −2 my taking causes lands in the other’s reward, which never enters my Q-values. So in every state taking is at least as good for me as leaving: it plays the role of D in the Prisoner’s Dilemma, a dominant action. When both agents do it, half the pickups are own coins (+1 in total) and half the other’s (+1−2=−1):

E[total reward per pickup]=21​(+1)+21​(−1)=0.

(A simultaneous pickup of one coin also totals 0: +1+1−2 over two pickups.)

(c) Two reasons work together. First, each Q-learner maximises its own return, and for that return restraint is costly: it gives up +1 now and protects only the other’s reward. It could pay only if it changed what the other does later (reciprocity, as Tit-for-Tat in Lab 1). Second, independent Q-learning treats the other agent as part of the environment: the other’s behaviour is just part of the transitions and rewards, and the state holds positions and the coin, with no record of whether the other has been restrained. So neither agent can reward restraint or punish a defector; in Lab 1’s terms both are memory-0 with respect to the partner, and against any fixed behaviour of the other, taking every coin is the best response. The result is mutual defection, as with the Part I learners in the Prisoner’s Dilemma. Restraint would need rewards that include the other’s welfare, memory of the other’s behaviour, or learners that account for how the other learns.

Q7: irrelevant lamps (prediction)

Statement

Each agent’s observation gets k lamps: signals switched on or off at random at every step, with no effect on the game. For a table, two observations differing in one lamp are different states: 1458⋅2k rows. Before running: how do you expect the individual performance of the Q-learners (coin pickups per episode) to change with 4 and with 8 lamps? Would the lamps bother a human player?

The best policy ignores the lamps, but a table cannot know that: it learns each lamp pattern separately. With a fixed amount of experience, 4 lamps should slow learning noticeably and 8 lamps (256 copies of every state) should leave the agents close to random. A human would not be bothered: after a few steps they would see that the lamps change whatever anyone does and ignore them, because people generalise across situations that differ only in irrelevant details.

The corrected model fits almost exactly (my runs visited every reachable state for 0 and 4 lamps, and 82.0% for 8).

(e) The budget is fixed while the table grows by 2k, so experience per row shrinks by the same factor. With 0 lamps each reachable state gets about 444 decisions, enough to learn its values. With 4 lamps every state is visited, but only about 28 times across four actions, and each update corrects 10% of the error while bootstrapping from under-trained next-state values, so values are only partly learned. With 8 lamps the average state is seen 1.7 times: 17% of test decisions land in a row never seen (random action), and most of the rest in rows seen once or twice, with values near 0. That is close to random play, hence 6.6 pickups.

(f) A table has one independent row per observation: nothing learned in one row is used in any other, so it cannot notice that 256 rows differing only in the lamps call for the same action. It must learn the same policy 256 times over, from 1/256 of the data each time. To be unaffected, a learner needs generalisation across states: a representation that shares parameters between similar observations and can learn to give the lamps no weight, such as Function Approximation with a neural network (Neural Network Function Approximation) or with features that leave the lamps out.

Part III: deep RL with PPO

The model

Deep reinforcement learning replaces the table with a neural network that takes the observation (36 numbers plus lamps) as input; all states share the weights, so learning in one state carries over to similar ones. The lab uses PPO (proximal policy optimisation), a policy gradient method: it learns the policy itself and changes its parameters to make actions with higher long-term reward more likely.

Actor and critic. PPO is an Actor-Critic method with two separate networks, each with two hidden layers of 64 tanh units:

  • the actor π(a∣s;ϕ) outputs one preference per action; a softmax turns them into probabilities (Softmax Policy) and the agent samples its action;
  • the critic V(s;θ) outputs one number, an estimate of the return from state s.

Advantage. The actor makes action at​ more likely in proportion to its advantage, how much better it did than the critic expected:

Adv(st​,at​)=rt​+γV(st+1​;θ)−V(st​;θ)

This is the one-step estimate. The code mixes horizons with Generalized Advantage Estimation: weight λ=0 gives the one-step estimate, λ=1 the whole rest of the episode (Monte Carlo); here λ=0.95. The critic is trained towards the target yt​=Adv(st​,at​)+V(st​;θ).

Clipped actor loss. Each batch, collected by the behaviour policy πβ​ (the policy before the update), is used for several gradient steps. The importance sampling weight ρ=π(a∣s;ϕ)/πβ​(a∣s) measures how far the policy has moved since, and the loss clips it:

L(ϕ)=−min(ρAdv, clip(ρ,1−ϵ,1+ϵ)Adv),ϵ=0.2.

Beyond 1±ϵ the clipped term stops rewarding further change, so each update stays in a trust region around πβ​.

Full update. Each minibatch minimises the actor loss, minus ce​=0.01 times the policy’s Entropy (entropy regularisation), plus cv​=0.5 times the critic loss (yt​−V(st​;θ))2. Adam with learning rate 0.003 runs 4 epochs of 4 minibatches over each batch of 512 decisions (16 gradient steps), with advantages normalised to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 within the batch and the gradient norm clipped at 0.5. Then the batch is thrown away (PPO is on-policy). Without a critic the advantage becomes the Monte Carlo return ut​ (the discounted sum of rewards from t to the end of the episode), which is REINFORCE.

In the Coin Game each agent runs its own PPO (independent PPO), with the other agent treated as part of the environment, exactly as the Q-learners did.

Q9: the pieces of PPO

Statement

(a) What does the critic add, compared with an algorithm that uses the Monte Carlo return of the whole episode (REINFORCE)?

(b) The Q-learners needed ε-greedy exploration. Where does a PPO agent’s exploration come from, and which term of the loss keeps it from disappearing too early?

(a) REINFORCE pushes every action up in proportion to the whole return that followed it. In a lucky episode even bad moves are reinforced, and the return mixes the effects of many later actions, coin placements and the other agent’s moves, so the gradient estimate has high variance. The critic adds:

  • a Baseline: subtracting V(st​) asks whether the action did better than usual from this state, which removes the part of the outcome that does not depend on the action and lowers the variance;
  • bootstrapping: with λ<1 the advantage uses V(st+1​) in place of the rest of the episode, so an action is judged mainly by its immediate consequences. This sharpens credit assignment and lowers variance further, at the price of some bias when the critic is wrong.

(The code’s batch normalisation of advantages subtracts the batch mean, a crude constant baseline, so its REINFORCE variant is not entirely without one.)

(b) From the policy itself: the agent samples from the actor’s probabilities, so every action with non-zero probability keeps being tried. Training pushes good actions towards probability 1, which would end exploration; the entropy regularisation term (−ce​ times the policy’s entropy) rewards spread-out probabilities, so the policy does not become deterministic too early.

Statement

(a) Give two experiments in this lab in which a faster or more capable learner led to a worse, or no better, collective outcome. Quote the numbers.

(b) Complete the sentence, with evidence from Parts II and III: “Deep RL solved …, but not …“.

(a)

  1. More training in the Coin Game. The tabular Q-learners’ welfare peaked at about +2.4 to +2.7 per episode around updates 100 to 200 (12 to 14 pickups, own share 0.60) and fell to +0.6 by update 1000 (16.9 pickups, share 0.52): better at the individual task, worse collectively, ending barely above random agents (about 0) with 4.6 times their pickups.
  2. PPO against the table with 8 lamps. PPO is the more capable learner, 12.8 to 13.3 pickups against 6.6, and its collective outcome is no better: own share 0.51 against 0.48, welfare +0.2 to +0.3 against −0.3, all close to 0.

(b) “Deep RL solved generalisation: 8 irrelevant lamps left PPO at its lamp-free level (12.8 to 13.3 pickups) while they cut the table from 16.9 to 6.6, but not the social dilemma: with either learner the own share stays near 0.5 and welfare near 0, because each agent’s own reward makes taking every coin the best response.”

(c) No. Equilibrium selection comes from the game together with learners that each follow their own payoff, and a network changes neither; with a single state there is nothing to generalise over. For a two-action softmax policy with logit difference z, πC​=σ(z) and the expected payoff is J=πC​fC​+(1−πC​)fD​, where fC​,fD​ are the expected payoffs of C and D against a partner who plays C with probability q. Since dσ/dz=σ(1−σ),

∂z∂J​=πC​(1−πC​)(fC​−fD​),fC​−fD​=q(R−T)+(1−q)(S−P)=3q−2.

Like Lab 1’s replicator equation x˙=x(1−x)(fC​−fD​), this pushes towards C exactly when q>2/3. A fresh network gives about πC​=1/2, so each agent sees q≈1/2, its gradient points towards D, and the pair drifts to (D,D), as the replicator dynamics do from x=1/2; both starting above 2/3 would lead to (C,C). Both pure equilibria stay stable (fC​−fD​=1 near q=1, −2 near q=0), so the outcome depends on initialisation and noise. Sampled actions and the entropy bonus keep the policies stochastic, which acts like exploration and, as in Q4, threatens (C,C) more than (D,D). Clipping limits the size of each step and leaves its direction unchanged.

Common pitfalls in Parts II and III

  • Reading an own share above 0.5 as cooperation. Own coins are worth more to a selfish agent (taking one also prevents a −2).
  • Counting all 9⋅9⋅9⋅2 rows as visitable. Only 1,152 of the 1,458 position states can occur.
  • “A better learner fixes the dilemma.” Function approximation fixes representation; the dilemma comes from the rewards.

Exam questions

Two independent stateless Q-learners ( α=0.1, ε-greedy) play the Stag Hunt with R=4, S=0, T=3, P=2, starting from Q=(0,0). Explain which equilibrium they usually reach and why, and how this compares with rational agents and with replicator dynamics.

Key points: random play at the start acts like belief 1/2 < p∗=2/3; D earns 2.5 against 2 and never below 2; (C,C) essentially only if both play C in round 1 (probability 1/4); feedback lock-in at (D,D), then exploration erodes the remaining (C,C); final (C,C) under 10%, below rational agents’ 11% and the replicator’s one third.

Start. All values are 0, so both agents break ties at random and each faces a coin-flipping partner. Then C earns 21​⋅4=2 and D earns 21​⋅3+21​⋅2=2.5 on average, and D never earns less than 2 while C earns 0 half the time. The start is like holding belief 1/2, below p∗=(P−S)/((R−T)+(P−S))=2/3.

Round 1 decides. If both play C, both get 4 and Q(C)=0.4>0: both prefer C. If either plays D, the D player’s Q(D) becomes positive, and a C player facing D earns 0, stays tied and drifts to D as soon as its tie-break picks D (2 against 0). So (C,C) is reached with probability about 1/4; my runs give 28% after the first rounds.

Lock-in. Once the partner plays D, C pays 0, so Q(C) never gets evidence of the 4 it could earn; Q(C) rises only when both play C together, (ε/2)2=0.25% of rounds. Meanwhile the partner’s exploratory defections push (C,C) runs over to (D,D) (each zero cuts Q(C) by 10%), and nothing comes back. After 3000 rounds (C,C) holds in roughly 5% to 10% of runs.

Comparison. Rational agents with uniform beliefs reached (C,C) in 11% of encounters, and replicator dynamics reached all-C from one third of starting states. Learning did not help: the learners start below p∗ and noise drives them towards the risk-dominant (D,D) (P−S=2>R−T=1).

Two stateless Q-learners in the Stag Hunt ( R=4,S=0,T=3,P=2, α=0.1) start at (C,C) with Q=(3.6,2.9). Explain why they leave (C,C) at ε=0.1 although C is the best response, and why the escape from (D,D) to (C,C) almost never happens.

Key points: C pays 3.8 and D 2.95 against an exploring partner; Q is an exponentially weighted average of recent payoffs (memory about 10 plays); three zeros take Q(C) from 3.8 to 2.77 < 2.95; the partner then earns 0 and follows; from (D,D) C pays 4 only on joint exploration (0.25% of rounds), about seven successes in a row needed, both must switch at once; basins of 1/3 against 2/3.

Best response. The partner plays D in ε/2=5% of rounds. C earns 0.95⋅4=3.8, D earns 0.95⋅3+0.05⋅2=2.95, so a rational agent stays.

What Q estimates. Unrolling the update gives Qt​(C)=α∑k​(1−α)krt−k​: an exponentially weighted average of recent payoffs, with weights halving about every 6.6 plays. With a constant α it tracks what C paid lately, with standard deviation σα/(2−α)​=0.20 around 3.8.

Escape. Each exploratory D of the partner pays me 0 and cuts Q(C) by 10%: 3.8→3.42→3.08→2.77<2.95. A short run of three such zeros, likely somewhere in 3000 rounds, flips my greedy action to D; then the partner earns 0 every round and flips within about three rounds. In my runs only 17% to 20% of pairs are at (C,C) after 3000 rounds (100% at ε=0.01, 0.4% or less at ε=0.5). One agent’s exploration is noise in the other’s rewards, and the learner reads it as a change in the partner.

No way back. At (D,D), C pays 0.05⋅4=0.2 on average and D 2.05. Q(C) changes only when I explore C, and pays 4 only if the partner explores C in the same round, 0.25% of rounds; failures are 19 times as common. Climbing from 0.2 to above 2 takes about seven successes in a row, and both agents must switch together or the switcher earns 0. Structurally, leaving (C,C) needs the partner to look like a defector more than 1−p∗=1/3 of the time, leaving (D,D) needs it to look like a cooperator more than p∗=2/3 of the time.

In the Coin Game a pickup pays the picker +1 whatever the coin's colour, and taking the other player's coin costs that player 2. Explain why two independent Q-learners collect many coins but do not show restraint, derive the expected total reward per pickup, and relate this to the Prisoner's Dilemma.

Key points: taking any reachable coin is at least as good for my own reward in every state; the −2 lands in the other’s reward, which my values never see; welfare = pickups × (2 × share − 1); expected total per pickup 0 for colour-blind agents; independent learners treat the other as environment, no memory of its behaviour, no reciprocity; same structure as mutual defection.

Dominance. Taking a coin pays me +1. Leaving the other’s coin pays me 0 now, and if the other then takes it I still get 0; the cost of my taking it, −2, goes to the other. So taking every reachable coin is a dominant behaviour for my own reward, the analogue of D.

Total per pickup. An own-colour pickup adds +1 to the total; a pickup of the other’s coin adds +1−2=−1. With colour-blind agents half of the pickups are of each kind, so the expected total is 21​−21​=0, and welfare =pickups×(2×restraint−1)≈0 however skilled they are.

Results. My Q-learners went from the random level (3.7 pickups) to about 17 pickups per episode, with own-colour share 0.52 and welfare about +0.6, against about 0 for random agents.

Why learning cannot fix it. Independent Q-learning treats the other agent as part of the environment and maximises its own return. Restraint costs +1 and benefits only the other, unless it changes the other’s future behaviour. But the state holds only positions and the coin, with no record of the other’s past restraint, so neither agent can reciprocate or punish; both are memory-0 with respect to the partner, and against any fixed behaviour of the other, taking every coin is the best response. As in the one-shot Prisoner’s Dilemma, individually rational learning ends in mutual defection.

Describe the components of PPO (actor, critic, advantage, clipped objective, entropy regularisation), say what the critic adds over REINFORCE, and explain why independent PPO in the Coin Game handles irrelevant inputs but still fails the social dilemma.

Key points: actor softmax policy sampled for exploration; critic V(s) as baseline and bootstrap for Adv=r+γV(s′)−V(s) (GAE with λ); clipped ratio ρ keeps updates in a trust region; entropy bonus prevents premature determinism; shared weights generalise across lamp patterns; own-colour share about 0.5 and welfare about 0 because each agent’s reward makes taking every coin the best response.

Actor. A network π(a∣s;ϕ) outputs action preferences turned into probabilities by a softmax; the agent samples, which is where exploration comes from.

Critic and advantage. A second network V(s;θ) estimates the return. The advantage Adv(st​,at​)=rt​+γV(st+1​)−V(st​) says how much better the action did than expected; generalized advantage estimation mixes horizons with λ (0 one-step, 1 Monte Carlo). Compared with REINFORCE, which pushes each action by the whole return that followed, the critic provides a baseline and bootstrapping, so the gradient has much lower variance and credit goes to the action’s own consequences, at the cost of some bias.

Clipped objective. Each batch is reused for several epochs. The ratio ρ=π(a∣s;ϕ)/πβ​(a∣s) measures how far the policy moved; the loss −min(ρAdv,clip(ρ,1−ϵ,1+ϵ)Adv) stops rewarding change beyond 1±ϵ, a trust region around the behaviour policy.

Entropy. The loss subtracts ce​ times the policy entropy, keeping probabilities spread so exploration does not stop too early.

Coin Game. The network shares weights across all observations, and random lamps carry no information about returns, so PPO learns to ignore them: in my runs 12.8 to 13.3 pickups with 8 lamps, as without, against 6.6 for the table. But each PPO agent still optimises its own reward with the other treated as environment, so taking every coin stays the best response: own-colour share about 0.51 and welfare near 0. Deep RL fixes representation and generalisation; the dilemma lives in the incentives.

Would replacing the Stag Hunt's Q-learners with PPO learners remove the problem of equilibrium selection? Use the expected policy gradient of a two-action softmax policy.

Key points: one state, nothing to generalise; ∂J/∂z=πC​(1−πC​)(fC​−fD​) with fC​−fD​=3q−2; like the replicator equation, it favours C only when q>2/3; from π=1/2 both drift to D; both pure equilibria stable, the outcome depends on initialisation and noise; sampling and entropy act like exploration and threaten (C,C) more.

Gradient. With logit difference z, πC​=σ(z) and J=πC​fC​+(1−πC​)fD​, where fC​=qR+(1−q)S and fD​=qT+(1−q)P for a partner who plays C with probability q. Since dσ/dz=σ(1−σ), ∂J/∂z=πC​(1−πC​)(fC​−fD​), and for R=4,S=0,T=3,P=2, fC​−fD​=q−2(1−q)=3q−2. Like the replicator equation x˙=x(1−x)(fC​−fD​), it pushes towards C exactly when q>2/3.

Outcome. A fresh network gives πC​≈1/2 to both agents, so each sees q≈1/2<2/3 and moves towards D; the pair goes to (D,D). Initial policies above 2/3 would go to (C,C). Both pure equilibria are stable (fC​−fD​=+1 near q=1, −2 near q=0), so which one is reached depends on where the policies start and on noise, exactly the problem Q-learners had.

Why the network does not help. The Stag Hunt has a single state, so generalisation across states, PPO’s advantage in the Coin Game, has nothing to work on; clipping limits the size of steps and leaves their direction unchanged. Sampled actions and the entropy bonus keep the policy stochastic, which, as with ε-greedy, makes (C,C) more fragile than (D,D). Equilibrium selection belongs to the game and to independent learners each following their own payoff.

Flashcards

What is the stateless Q-learning update?

Q(a)←Q(a)+α(r−Q(a)), only for the action just played.

Why can the γmaxa′​Q(s′,a′) term be dropped in a repeated stage game?

With one state it adds the same amount to both actions’ targets, so it never changes which is greedy.

With a constant learning rate α, what does Q(a) estimate?

An exponentially weighted average of recent payoffs of a, memory about 1/α plays.

What does "independent learning" mean for two agents?

Each learns from its own actions and rewards only, treating the other as part of the environment.

Why is the environment of an independent learner non-stationary?

The other agent is learning too, so the payoff each action earns keeps changing.

Against a coin-flipping partner in the Stag Hunt ( R=4,S=0,T=3,P=2), what do C and D earn on average?

C earns 2, D earns 2.5.

Independent Q-learners start at Q=(0,0) in the Stag Hunt. When do they reach (C,C)?

Essentially only if both play C in round 1, probability about 1/4.

About how often do independent Q-learners from Q=(0,0), ε=0.1, end at (C,C) in the Stag Hunt after 3000 rounds?

Under 10% of runs; the rest end at (D,D).

Why do Stag Hunt Q-learners lock into (D,D) so fast?

Once the partner plays D, C pays 0, so Q(C) never sees evidence that it could pay 4.

Why is (D,D) reached by Prisoner's Dilemma learners not a coordination failure?

D is dominant and (D,D) the unique Nash equilibrium; the problem is incentives, a social dilemma.

With belief-based initial values in the Stag Hunt, when does an agent start with C?

When Q(C)−Q(D)=3b−2>0, i.e. belief b>p∗=2/3.

An optimist (belief 0.9) meets a sceptic (belief 0.5) as Q-learners in the Stag Hunt. Who adapts?

The optimist: its C earns 0 and Q(C) drops 10% per round; it switches in round 4.

Why does the sceptic never adapt to the optimist in the Stag Hunt?

It never plays C, so Q(C) is never updated, and its D is rewarded with 3 or 2.

Why does the optimist relapse to C a few times after switching to D?

At (D,D) its Q(D) falls towards 2 while its stale Q(C) is still above 2.

Against a partner exploring 10% of the time at (C,C), what do C and D pay in the Stag Hunt?

C pays 3.8, D pays 2.95.

Three: 3.8→3.42→3.08→2.77.

Why do Q-learners leave (C,C) in the Stag Hunt even though C is the best response?

The partner’s exploration gives occasional zeros, and recency-weighted Q reads a short bad streak as a change.

How does (C,C) survival in the Stag Hunt change as ε rises from 0.01 to 0.5?

It falls: survives at 0.01, mostly at 0.05, mostly lost at 0.1, gone within rounds at 0.5.

Why is escaping from (D,D) to (C,C) far rarer than the reverse for Q-learners?

C pays 4 only on joint exploration, Q(C) must climb from about 0.2 to 2, and both must switch together.

In basin terms, why is (C,C) more fragile than (D,D) in the Stag Hunt with p∗=2/3?

Leaving (C,C) needs the partner to defect over 1/3 of the time; leaving (D,D) needs cooperation over 2/3.

In the Coin Game, what does a pickup of the other player's coin do to the rewards?

The picker gets +1 and the coin’s owner gets −2.

What is the welfare formula of the Coin Game?

Welfare = pickups × (2 × own-colour share − 1).

What is the expected total reward per pickup when both Coin Game agents are colour-blind?

0: half the pickups add +1, half add −1.

Why does a self-interested Coin Game agent take every reachable coin?

Taking pays +1 whatever the colour; the −2 lands in the other’s reward, never in its own.

Why can independent Q-learners not learn restraint in the Coin Game?

Restraint only helps the other, and their state holds no record of the other’s behaviour, so no reciprocity.

Why is an own-colour share of about 0.6 in the Coin Game not evidence of cooperation?

A selfish agent gains 3 by taking its own coin before the other does, only 1 for the other’s coin.

How did tabular Q-learners' Coin Game welfare change with more training?

It peaked early (about +2.5) and fell to about +0.6 as pickups kept rising.

How many rows does the Coin Game Q-table have with k irrelevant lamps?

1458⋅2k.

Why can only 1,152 of the 1,458 Coin Game position states occur at decision time?

The coin is never on a cell occupied by an agent.

Under the uniform-visit assumption, what is the probability a state is never visited in B decisions?

(1−1/N)B≈e−B/N.

What is the expected number of visited states among N after B uniform decisions?

N(1−e−B/N).

What training budget do the Coin Game runs use with the code's default of 1000 updates?

B=1000×32×16=512,000 decisions per agent.

Why do irrelevant lamps ruin a tabular Q-learner with a fixed budget?

Each lamp pattern is a separate row, so experience per row falls by 2k and nothing transfers.

What does a learner need to be unaffected by irrelevant inputs?

Generalisation: parameters shared across observations, able to give irrelevant inputs no weight.

What are the actor and the critic in PPO?

Actor: softmax policy network. Critic: network estimating the state value V(s).

What is the one-step advantage estimate in actor-critic methods?

Adv=rt​+γV(st+1​)−V(st​).

What does the GAE parameter λ control in PPO?

The horizon of the advantage estimate: 0 one-step, 1 Monte Carlo.

What does PPO's clipping of the importance ratio ρ do?

Stops rewarding policy change beyond 1±ϵ, keeping each update in a trust region.

What does the critic add compared with REINFORCE?

A baseline and bootstrapping, which lower the variance of the gradient and sharpen credit assignment.

Where does a PPO agent's exploration come from?

Sampling actions from its stochastic softmax policy.

Which term of PPO's loss keeps exploration from disappearing too early?

The entropy regularisation term.

Why is PPO hardly affected by 8 irrelevant lamps in the Coin Game?

Its network shares weights across observations and learns to give the random lamps no influence.

Does independent PPO solve the Coin Game's social dilemma?

No: own-colour share stays about 0.5 and welfare about 0, as with Q-learners.

Complete: "Deep RL solved ..., but not ..." for the Coin Game.

Generalisation over irrelevant inputs, but not the social dilemma.

When is a Q-table still a better choice than a neural network?

Small discrete state spaces visited often, or when exact, inspectable values are needed.

Why would PPO learners in the Stag Hunt still face equilibrium selection?

Their payoff gradient ∝3q−2 points to D from a uniform start; both equilibria stay stable.

True or false: in the Stag Hunt, more exploration makes independent Q-learners coordinate on (C,C) more often.

False. Exploration destroys (C,C) and leaves (D,D) intact.
