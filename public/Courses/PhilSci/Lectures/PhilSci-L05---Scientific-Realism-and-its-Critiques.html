PhilSci-L05: Scientific Realism and its Critiques

In short

Physics says electrons exist, though nobody has ever seen one. Should you believe in them, or only that the theory gets every observation right?

Scientific realists: believe in them. Our best theories are true or approximately true, unobservables included, or their success at predicting brand-new results would be a miracle.

Van Fraassen: accepting a theory commits you only to believing it gets the observable world right. Belief in its electrons is up to you.

Laudan: history is full of successful theories built on things that do not exist (the aether, phlogiston), so success is no sign of truth.

On the mock exam this is Q8 (10 marks): Musgrave’s reply to van Fraassen’s attack on the realist’s demand for explanation, covered under van Fraassen below.

Where the word “realism” comes from

Is the world there when nobody looks?

The oldest form of the question is about what exists (the ontological question): does the external world exist independently of us, of our thoughts and perceptions?

  • Metaphysical realism says yes: objects exist independently of the mind. Many philosophers, from Plato on, hold some version of it.
  • Idealism says no: the world depends on the mind. George Berkeley (18th-century Irish philosopher) put it as esse est percipi, “to be is to be perceived”: what nobody perceives does not exist.

Idealism is less silly than it sounds. Perception is our only access to anything outside ourselves, so we can never check whether anything exists beyond it: idealism is “the maximally empiricist possible answer” (empiricism: all knowledge comes from experience).

Kant: the world exists, but we only know how it appears

Immanuel Kant (18th-century German philosopher) found a middle ground between realism and idealism by switching to a question about knowledge (the epistemological question): what can we know of the external world?

There is an external reality, the things-in-themselves or noumena (the chair as it is in itself), but we cannot know it. We can only know appearances, the phenomena (the chair as it appears to me).

Phenomena are shaped by structures the mind brings to every experience: space and time (Kant’s “forms of intuition”) and categories such as causality and possibility. Since the input still comes from the world, knowing phenomena is knowing the world as it appears through the mind’s filter, never as it is in itself.

Eddington’s two tables: science sharpens the worry

Science asks the same question one level down, because it describes things nobody can perceive at all. In 1927 the physicist Arthur Eddington (who led the 1919 eclipse expedition in L01b) described his two tables. The everyday one is coloured, permanent, “above all it is substantial”. The scientific one is “mostly emptiness”, sparsely scattered with electric charges rushing about.

Which table is real? The solid one, the nearly empty one, or both, and how are they related? This is the realism debate in its modern form: which of the things science describes are we committed to?

Mach and the positivists: only the observable counts

Ernst Mach (Austrian physicist and philosopher) took the extreme empiricist line, radical empiricism:

  • Claims about unobservable reality are metaphysical and cannot have a truth-value: if your senses cannot check a claim, there is no deciding it.
  • Theories are merely economical descriptions of our sensory experiences, tools for ordering past experiences and predicting new ones. Treating theories as tools in this way is instrumentalism.

The early logical positivists, Carnap and Neurath in particular (see L01), went one step further and tied meaning, as well as truth, to observation. Their picture: a theoretical sentence is shorthand for observational ones. “There are electrons in this tube” means only something like “the meter will read this, the screen will glow there”. Read literally, as a claim that unobservable things exist, it says nothing. Hence:

  • Verifiability theory of meaning: claims about the existence of unobservable entities cannot be verified, so they are meaningless.
  • A sharp theory-observation distinction: an exact line between observational terms and sentences (about what we can see) and theoretical ones (about what we cannot).
  • Theoretical sentences are introduced through observational sentences, and get whatever meaning they have from those links.

The line has to be sharp because meaning does not come in degrees: no sentence is “almost meaningful”.

Later members of the movement, usually called logical empiricists, drifted towards realism: they accepted semantics (theoretical sentences can be true or false) and replaced verification with confirmation (a claim counts if evidence can support it, a much weaker demand).

Maxwell: the line cannot be drawn

In 1962 Grover Maxwell, an American philosopher of science, attacked the line itself.

From glasses to microscopes

Looking through glasses is plainly observing: putting them on does not move you into the theoretical realm. Looking through a window is observing too. But glasses are not different in principle from a microscope or a telescope, which are only more elaborate arrangements of lenses. Yet a microscope image does not seem to count as observation the way glasses do. In between lies a continuum of possible instruments, and no principled point where observation stops.

Maxwell's objection (1962)

The theory-observation distinction cannot be sharply drawn.

This first realist attack was effective: if meaning depends on a line nobody can draw, there is no saying which claims are meaningful. With the line in trouble, scientific realism became the position to beat.

What scientific realism claims

Scientific realism

Scientific theories that are empirically well-confirmed are true or approximately true.

“True” here includes what the theory says about unobservables, such as particles and viruses. Compare the weaker claim:

Empirical adequacy

A theory is empirically adequate when it represents, describes and predicts the observable phenomena well: what it says about the observable is true.

That covers all observable phenomena, including ones nobody has observed yet. Realism goes further: the realist also believes the unobservable parts.

Bas van Fraassen, the leading modern anti-realist (below), made this precise in 1980: a theory is empirically adequate “if and only if it has at least one model such that all appearances are isomorphic to empirical substructures of that model”. In plain words: part of some model of the theory (the empirical substructures) lines up exactly with every observable phenomenon (the appearances), structure preserved (isomorphic). The rest of the model, about unobservables, does not matter.

Language, knowledge, existence: the parts of realism

The definition packs in three claims: read the theory as talking about electrons, think we are entitled to believe it, and hold that it is true. A scientific realist accepts all three. An anti-realist is anyone who rejects at least one.

The kinds of scientific realism

  • Semantic realism (about language): interpret scientific theories literally. Theories aim to tell a “literal story” about how the world works. The contrast is the early logical positivists’ construal of theoretical terms as shorthand for observations.
  • Epistemic realism (about knowledge): one is justified in accepting scientific theories as true.
  • Metaphysical realism (about what there is): the theory is true or approximately true. The world is like the theory says it is, and its entities exist.

Semantic and metaphysical realism are easy to blur. Semantic realism says “there are electrons” is a literal claim, true or false. Metaphysical realism says it is true: there really are electrons.

Two meanings of "metaphysical realism"

Against Berkeley, it means an external world exists independently of the mind. Inside scientific realism, it means the theory is (approximately) true and its entities exist. Everyone in the scientific realism debate accepts (or sets aside) the first. The fight is over the second.

The case for realism: success would otherwise be a miracle

The no-miracles argument

Particle physics predicted the top quark and the Higgs boson from theory, before any experiment on them. Both are unobservable, and both were then found. If the theories were false, this would be an incredible coincidence, and science pulls it off again and again.

No-miracles argument (NMA), from the American philosopher Hilary Putnam

“Realism is the only philosophy that does not make the success of science a miracle.”

If scientific theories were not truth-tracking (reliably latching on to how the world is), it is hard to see how they could make successful predictions of experiments never performed before.

The weight rests on novel predictions. Fitting the observations made so far is guaranteed for a theory built to fit them; getting right observations nobody had made yet is not. Put as a question to the anti-realist: why would a theory be empirically adequate, future observations included, unless it were true?

Inference to the best explanation

The no-miracles argument is an inference to the best explanation (IBE), also called abduction:

  • What needs explaining (the explanandum): theories successfully predict the outcomes of experiments never performed before.
  • Best explanation: the theories are approximately true.
  • Conclusion: theories that reach a certain level of success in prediction and testing are (probably) approximately true.

IBE is also the realist’s second argument in its own right. If believing the best explanation is a good general rule of reasoning, then we should believe the theories that best explain the phenomena, unobservables included. The no-miracles argument applies the same rule to the success of science as a whole.

Inference to the best explanation

Given evidence E and candidate explanations H1​,…,Hn​ of E, infer the truth of that Hi​ which best explains E.

Here E is the evidence (for the realist: the success of science), H1​,…,Hn​ are rival hypotheses that would each explain it, and Hi​ is the best of them (the theory is approximately true).

Like induction, IBE is ampliative (the conclusion goes beyond what is logically contained in the premises) and fallible. Deduction is neither: it is truth-preserving (true premises guarantee a true conclusion). Induction appeals to observed frequencies; IBE also appeals to explanatory considerations.

That is where the anti-realist pushes back: many anti-realists do not accept explanation as an aim of science. On van Fraassen’s view from L04, an explanation is an answer to a why-question, and what counts as a good answer depends on the context of who asks and why. Explaining is a use of science for our own purposes, so “best explanation” gives no reason to believe a theory true. Van Fraassen also argues that explanation is not an aim of science at all; Musgrave’s reply to that comes below.

Van Fraassen’s alternative: constructive empiricism

Van Fraassen built the most developed alternative to realism, constructive empiricism. He states both positions in the same two-part form.

Scientific realism, in van Fraassen's statement

“Science aims to give us, in its theories, a literally true story of what the world is like; and acceptance of a scientific theory involves the belief that it is true.”

Note “literally true”: most realists claim only approximate truth, a point they press in L06.

Constructive empiricism

“Science aims to give us theories which are empirically adequate; and acceptance of a theory involves as belief only that it is empirically adequate.”

They differ twice. On the aim of science: a literally true story, or empirical adequacy. On what accepting a theory commits you to believing: that it is true, or only that what it says about the observable is true.

Where he stands on the three theses:

  • Semantic realist. Claims about unobservables are literally true or false, so meaningful.
  • Epistemic and metaphysical anti-realist. Success does not oblige you to believe what a theory says about unobservables.
  • Agnostic. “Whether we believe what science says about unobservable entities is up to us.” He does not say electrons do not exist; he says the evidence does not force you to believe they do.

Side by side:

SemanticEpistemicMetaphysical
Scientific realistYesYesYes
Mach, early positivistsNoNoNo
Van FraassenYesNoNo (agnostic)

So the disagreement with the realist is only about unobservable entities. He then answers the realist’s arguments in turn: Maxwell’s case against the theory-observation distinction, IBE, the demand for explanation, and the no-miracles argument, which he calls the Ultimate Argument (unrelated to Musgrave’s “ultimate explanation” below).

Reply to Maxwell: the line can be vague

Maxwell’s argument was deadly for the positivists because they hung meaning on the line, and meaning is all-or-nothing. Existence is all-or-nothing too, so an anti-realist who said “only observable things exist” would hit the same problem. Van Fraassen never says that. He escapes in three moves.

  1. Take theories literally in all respects. Drop the verifiability theory of meaning. “Electrons exist” is meaningful, true or false. Whether we are justified in believing it is a separate question.
  2. The theory-observation distinction is a category mistake (treating a thing as the wrong kind of thing). It runs together a distinction in language, theoretical versus non-theoretical terms, and one in objects, observable versus unobservable entities. Once they are separated, the observable/unobservable line is about which things we can see. It says nothing about which words are meaningful, and a theoretical term can describe an observable thing.
  3. “Observable” is a vague but usable predicate. Like “bald”: no one hair makes the difference, yet there are clear cases on each side.

Why it works: van Fraassen hangs neither meaning nor existence on the line, only what we are obliged to believe (our epistemic commitment). For belief a vague boundary is harmless: clear cases on both sides (a table, an electron) are all he needs. So Maxwell refutes the positivist and misses van Fraassen. Whether a vague line is good enough is still disputed: where “observable” ends is an open problem for constructive empiricism in L06.

Reply to IBE: a rival hypothesis

The realist says we follow a rule: believe the best explanation is true. Van Fraassen calls this a psychological hypothesis about how we reason, and offers a rival hypothesis that fits our practice equally well: we are always willing to believe that the theory which best explains the evidence is empirically adequate. Only the realist’s version adds belief in unobservables. So our practice does not force realism, and constructive empiricism is a viable alternative.

The demand for explanation: Musgrave’s reply

Realists want observed regularities explained, by unobservable structure if need be: gases behave as they do because of molecules. Besides calling explanation pragmatic, van Fraassen attacks this realist demand for explanation and argues that explanation is not one of the aims of science. As Musgrave’s reply reads it, the attack treats the demand as unlimited: every explanation leaves something unexplained (why do molecules behave as they do?), so the demand can never be met, and science does not insist on it.

The realist Alan Musgrave replies that van Fraassen “tacitly conflates ‘realism with essentialism, … the demand for explanation with the demand for ultimate explanation’“. The reply rests on one distinction:

  • An ultimate explanation explains something in terms that need no further explanation. It removes puzzlement: nothing is left to ask. Essentialism is the view that science seeks this kind of explanation, bottoming out in the essences of things.
  • A non-ultimate explanation explains one thing by another that itself still needs explaining. It does not remove puzzlement: it relocates it (the puzzle is now about the molecules) and can even enhance it (the molecules raise more questions than the gas did). It is still a real explanation.

So van Fraassen’s argument works against essentialism and does not touch realism.

Musgrave's reply, in the official key's words

Ultimate scientific explanations serve to remove puzzlement. Non-ultimate scientific explanations do not serve this pragmatic function of removing puzzlement (they relocate it and enhance it), yet they are real explanations. In other words, there can always be better or deeper explanations, and scientific theories certainly do aim to give explanations, but there is no unlimited demand for explanations.

Why this is a reply. Van Fraassen’s case works only if “explanation” means ultimate explanation. Once non-ultimate explanations count as genuine, science can aim at ever better and deeper explanations without an unlimited demand. That removes one of van Fraassen’s reasons for setting aside the realist’s appeal to the best explanation. His rival hypothesis about IBE is a separate objection, and Musgrave’s distinction does not touch it.

Reply to the no-miracles argument: survival of the fittest

Van Fraassen’s own answer to the realist’s question of why a theory would be empirically adequate unless true: theories compete and only the successful ones survive, “the ones which in fact latched on to actual regularities in nature”. As in Darwin, selection explains the fit. Success needs no truth behind it.

The case against: Laudan’s pessimistic meta-induction

The most influential argument against realism is historical: Larry Laudan’s (American philosopher of science, met in L03), from “A Confutation of Convergent Realism” (1981). Its target is convergent realism, the view that science gets closer and closer to the truth.

The argument

The aether was the medium light waves supposedly travelled through; optics and electromagnetism were built on it, and predicted well. Phlogiston was the substance supposedly released when things burn; chemistry was built on it. We now think neither exists. A term refers when there is something in the world it picks out: “electron” refers if there are electrons.

Pessimistic meta-induction (PMI)

  1. History of science: many theories that we now consider false were once empirically successful.
  2. These theories assumed that terms like “aether” and “phlogiston” referred to entities.
  3. We now take those terms not to refer: nothing in the world corresponds to them.
  4. A theory whose central terms do not refer cannot be true, or even approximately true. So these successful theories were false.
  5. Induction: presently successful theories will (may well) turn out to be false too. We are not justified in believing that theories are true.
  6. So there is no reason to suppose that the entities, properties and processes postulated by our best theories are real.

Step 4 is where reference matters: there is no aether, so the aether theory was not approximately true, yet it was successful.

It is a meta-induction because it generalises over theories, one level up from induction over observations.

The assumption doing the work: our situation is no different from that of scientists in the 1800s, who were just as confident and turned out wrong. Thinking that we are finally the ones who got it right would be a kind of arrogance.

Laudan’s list

Laudan backs the first premise with a list he calls “the historical gambit”: the crystalline spheres of ancient and medieval astronomy, the humoral theory of medicine, the effluvial theory of static electricity, catastrophist geology (with its universal deluge), the phlogiston theory of chemistry, the caloric and the vibratory theories of heat, vital force theories of physiology, the electromagnetic aether, the optical aether, the theory of circular inertia, and theories of spontaneous generation.

“This list, which could be extended ad nauseam, involves in every case a theory which was once successful and well confirmed, but which contained central terms which (we now believe) were non-referring.”

The target: convergent realism

Convergent realism

Theories are approximately true, and successive theories get closer and closer to the truth: they converge on it.

Roughly, a theory is approximately true when it gets the world’s most central features right while being wrong in details (Psillos’s informal gloss, from the week 6 reading). Laudan’s complaint is that nobody has made this precise.

Convergent realism has three elements:

  1. Approximate truth: mature theories are close to the truth, and the more mature, the closer.
  2. Reference: mature theories refer, and preserve reference across theory change. Otherwise successive theories would be about different things, with nothing to converge on.
  3. Empirical success: new theories explain why the old ones were successful.

Laudan attacks the links between these three elements, in three related problems.

Laudan’s problems with approximate truth

  1. There is no good definition of “approximate truth”. “Few of the writers of whom I am aware have defined what it means for a statement or theory to be ‘approximately true’.” And truth, like meaning, seems all-or-nothing, so it is hard to say what being true “to a degree” could mean.
  2. Nothing justifies the link between empirical success and reference that realism assumes. The realist’s route runs success → approximate truth → reference: success is the reason to think a theory approximately true, and a theory can only be approximately true if its central terms refer. Approximate truth is too vague to work with, so the realist is left with reference. But reference touches success only through that vague middle step, so the link is extremely tenuous. It fails in both directions in practice. A theory can refer and still be unsuccessful: in the old theory of tectonic plates, “tectonic plates” referred, yet the theory was unsuccessful (probably the early theory of continental drift, rejected for decades). And a theory can succeed without referring, as aether theories did.
  3. Central terms of once-successful theories do not refer, so those theories were not approximately true. Aether and phlogiston theories were successful anyway. So success does not indicate approximate truth.

All three come back to approximate truth. The realist needs it to carry the argument. It is extremely hard to make sense of, and the historical cases may refute it outright.

Saatsi: two ways to read the PMI

The philosopher Juha Saatsi (2005) points out that the PMI’s conclusion can be read two ways.

  1. An induction on the past: past successful theories were false, so current theories are likely to be false too. A strong prediction about today’s science.
  2. A timeless argument that undermines the no-miracles argument: the past cases show that success is not a reliable indicator of truth. This attacks the realist’s reason for optimism and predicts nothing about current theories.

Reading 2 goes straight for the no-miracles argument (the best explanation of our current theories’ success is that they are true). The realist sees a string of successes and infers truth; Laudan points to earlier successful theories whose posits were not there. So the “miracles” look less miraculous, and even great success may not be enough to infer truth.

Reading 2 is more modest, and harder to resist. A realist can argue that today’s science is better placed than that of 1800, which weakens reading 1 but does nothing against reading 2. The realist’s replies are in L06.

Exam questions

Mock exam Q8: "One of the arguments discussed by van Fraassen and Musgrave, in their debate on scientific realism, is about scientific explanation. Van Fraassen attacks the realist demand for explanation, and argues that explanation is not one of the aims of science. Musgrave then responds that van Fraassen tacitly conflates 'realism with essentialism, ... the demand for explanation with the demand for ultimate explanation'. What does Musgrave mean by this, and how is it a reply to van Fraassen?" (10 points)

Key points: ultimate explanations remove puzzlement; non-ultimate explanations relocate and enhance puzzlement yet are real explanations; better or deeper explanations always possible; science does aim to explain; no unlimited demand for explanation.

Musgrave’s point, following the official key:

  1. Ultimate scientific explanations serve to remove puzzlement.
  2. Non-ultimate scientific explanations do not serve this pragmatic function: they relocate and enhance puzzlement. Yet they are real explanations.
  3. There can always be better or deeper explanations, and scientific theories certainly do aim to give explanations, but there is no unlimited demand for explanations.

How it answers van Fraassen: van Fraassen’s attack treats the realist’s demand for explanation as unlimited, a demand that can never be met because every explanation leaves something unexplained, and concludes that explanation is not an aim of science. That only follows if “explanation” means ultimate explanation, the essentialist ideal of explanation bottoming out in essences. Since non-ultimate explanations are genuine, science can aim at better and deeper explanations without any unlimited demand for ultimate ones. So the attack hits essentialism and misses realism. Explanation is an aim of science after all, and one of van Fraassen’s reasons for dismissing IBE falls away (his rival hypothesis about IBE is a separate objection).

What does scientific realism claim, and what are the two main arguments for it? Present them and say where an anti-realist pushes back.

Key points: well-confirmed theories approximately true, including about unobservables; conjunction of semantic, epistemic, metaphysical realism; no-miracles argument (NMA) with Higgs and top quark; IBE, of which the NMA is an instance, as a general rule; pushback: explanation not an aim, rival hypothesis, selection, PMI.

  • Claim: empirically well-confirmed theories are true or approximately true, including what they say about unobservables. This goes beyond empirical adequacy.
  • It is the conjunction of three theses: semantic (read theories literally), epistemic (we are justified in accepting them as true), metaphysical (the world is as the theory says; its entities exist). Rejecting any one makes you an anti-realist.
  • No-miracles argument (Putnam): “Realism is the only philosophy that does not make the success of science a miracle.” Novel predictions such as the Higgs boson and the top quark, made on theoretical grounds before any experiment, would be incredible coincidences if the theories were false.
  • IBE: the NMA is an inference to the best explanation. What needs explaining: theories successfully predict experiments never performed. Best explanation: their approximate truth. Conclusion: sufficiently successful theories are (probably) approximately true.
  • IBE is also an argument in its own right: if believing the best explanation is a good general rule, we should believe the theories that best explain the phenomena, unobservables included. The NMA applies that rule to the success of science as a whole.
  • Pushback: anti-realists often deny explanation is an aim of science, so “best explanation” gives no reason to believe. Van Fraassen offers a rival hypothesis (we believe the best explanation is empirically adequate). He also answers the NMA with selection: only successful theories survive, so success needs no truth. Laudan’s PMI shows success has gone with falsity before.

State van Fraassen's constructive empiricism and explain how it survives Maxwell's objection that the theory-observation distinction cannot be drawn sharply.

Key points: aim is empirical adequacy, acceptance is belief only in adequacy; semantic realist, epistemic anti-realist, agnostic about unobservables; Maxwell’s glasses-microscope continuum; category mistake: terms versus entities; “observable” vague but usable, only belief hangs on it.

  • Constructive empiricism: “Science aims to give us theories which are empirically adequate; and acceptance of a theory involves as belief only that it is empirically adequate.” Contrast realism: the aim is “a literally true story”, and acceptance involves belief that the theory is true.
  • Position: semantic realist (claims about unobservables are literally true or false), epistemic and metaphysical anti-realist. Belief about unobservables is “up to us”. He is agnostic about electrons; he does not say they do not exist.
  • Maxwell’s threat: glasses, window, microscope lie on a continuum with no principled cut-off. Fatal to the positivist, who tied meaning (all-or-nothing) to the line.
  • Three moves: (1) take theories literally in all respects, dropping verifiability; (2) the distinction is a category mistake: theoretical/non-theoretical terms (language) versus observable/unobservable entities (objects); (3) “observable” is vague but usable, like “bald”.
  • Why it works: only epistemic commitment hangs on the line, so a vague boundary is harmless. Clear cases on each side (a table, an electron) are all the argument needs. Where “observable” ends remains disputed.

Losing marks: calling van Fraassen a positivist, or saying he denies unobservables exist.

Present Laudan's pessimistic meta-induction against convergent realism, and explain Saatsi's two readings of it.

Key points: convergent realism: approximate truth, preserved reference, explained success; PMI: successful theories with non-referring terms (aether, phlogiston), induction over theories; three problems rooted in approximate truth; Saatsi reading 1: current theories likely false; Saatsi reading 2: success not a reliable indicator of truth.

  • Target, convergent realism: mature theories are approximately true, refer and preserve reference across theory change, and new theories explain the success of old ones, so science converges on the truth.
  • PMI: many once empirically successful theories are now considered false; they took terms like “aether” and “phlogiston” to refer, and we now take those terms not to refer. A theory whose central terms do not refer cannot be approximately true, so these successful theories were false. By induction over theories, present successful theories may well turn out false too, so we are not justified in believing theories true or their posits real.
  • Three problems (Laudan 1981): (1) no good definition of approximate truth; (2) no justification for the realist’s link success → approximate truth → reference; (3) central terms of once-successful theories do not refer, so those theories were not approximately true, yet were successful. All three stem from approximate truth.
  • Saatsi (2005): reading 1, an induction on the past (current theories are likely false); reading 2, a timeless argument that undermines the NMA by showing success is not a reliable indicator of truth. Reading 2 is more modest and harder to resist: even if our situation differs from the 1800s, past successful-but-false theories make the NMA’s “miracles” less miraculous.

Losing marks: treating the PMI as an induction over observations, or as just “science has been wrong before” without the target link success → approximate truth → reference.

A theory built to fit past observations is bound to fit them; getting right observations nobody has made yet is not guaranteed.

What question does the realist put to van Fraassen about empirical adequacy?

Why would a theory be empirically adequate, future observations included, unless it were true?

In the no-miracles argument read as an IBE, what is the best explanation of what?

Approximate truth of theories best explains their success predicting experiments never performed before.

What are the two main arguments for scientific realism?

The no-miracles argument and inference to the best explanation.

How is IBE an argument for realism in its own right?

If believing the best explanation is a good rule, believe the theories that best explain the phenomena, unobservables included.

How does IBE differ from deduction?

IBE is ampliative and fallible. Deduction is neither: it is truth-preserving, true premises guaranteeing a true conclusion.

How does IBE differ from induction?

Both are ampliative and fallible. Induction appeals to observed frequencies; IBE also appeals to explanatory considerations.

What makes an inference ampliative?

Its conclusion goes beyond what is logically contained in the premises.

Why does IBE fail to move many anti-realists, such as van Fraassen?

They do not accept explanation as an aim of science, so “best explanation” gives no reason to believe.

How does van Fraassen state constructive empiricism?

“Science aims to give us theories which are empirically adequate; and acceptance of a theory involves as belief only that it is empirically adequate.”

In which two respects do van Fraassen's formulations of realism and constructive empiricism differ?

The aim of science (literal truth or empirical adequacy) and what acceptance commits you to believing (truth or only empirical adequacy).

What rival hypothesis does van Fraassen offer to the realist's account of IBE?

We believe the theory that best explains the evidence is empirically adequate, which fits our practice equally well.

How does van Fraassen explain the success of science without appeal to truth?

Darwinian selection: theories compete, and only the successful ones survive.

What are van Fraassen's three moves against Maxwell's objection?

Take theories literally, the distinction is a category mistake, “observable” is vague but usable.

Which two distinctions does van Fraassen say the theory-observation distinction runs together?

Theoretical versus non-theoretical terms (language) and observable versus unobservable entities (objects).

Why does a vague observable/unobservable line refute the logical positivist but not van Fraassen?

The positivist hung meaning, all-or-nothing, on it. Van Fraassen hangs only epistemic commitment on it, where clear cases suffice.

What, according to Musgrave, do ultimate explanations do?

They remove puzzlement.

According to Musgrave, are non-ultimate explanations real explanations?

Yes, although they relocate and enhance puzzlement instead of removing it.

What does Musgrave say about the demand for explanation in science?

Theories do aim to explain, and better or deeper explanations are always possible, but there is no unlimited demand for explanation.

What does Musgrave accuse van Fraassen of conflating?

Realism with essentialism, and the demand for explanation with the demand for ultimate explanation.

What view is essentialism, in Musgrave's charge against van Fraassen?

That science seeks ultimate explanations, bottoming out in essences that need no further explanation.

Why is Musgrave's distinction a reply to van Fraassen's attack on the demand for explanation?

The attack works only against ultimate explanation. Science can still aim at non-ultimate explanations, so explanation remains an aim.

Why does it matter for realism whether explanation is an aim of science?

If it is, one of van Fraassen’s reasons for dismissing IBE falls away. His rival hypothesis still stands.

When does a term refer?

When there is something in the world it picks out.

In the pessimistic meta-induction, why does a non-referring central term make a theory false?

Its central claims are about something that does not exist, so they cannot be even approximately true.

What historical premise does Laudan's pessimistic meta-induction start from?

Many theories now considered false were once empirically successful.

Which terms are the standard examples of non-referring central terms in Laudan's pessimistic meta-induction?

“Aether” and “phlogiston”.

What does Laudan's pessimistic meta-induction conclude?

We are not justified in believing our best theories true, or that their posited entities are real.

Why is Laudan's pessimistic meta-induction a meta-induction?

The induction runs over theories, not over observations.

What assumption about our epistemic situation does the pessimistic meta-induction rely on?

We have no reason to think it differs from that of past scientists, who were just as sure and turned out wrong.

What is convergent realism?

Theories are approximately true, and successive theories get closer and closer to the truth.

What are the three elements of convergent realism that Laudan attacks?

Approximate truth, reference preserved across theory change, empirical success of old theories explained by new ones.

Why must convergent realism hold that reference is preserved across theory change?

Otherwise successive theories would be about different things, with nothing to converge on.

What are Laudan's three problems for convergent realism?

No good definition of approximate truth, no justification for the success-reference link, central terms of once-successful theories do not refer.

What do Laudan's three problems for convergent realism have in common?

They all stem from approximate truth, load-bearing for the realist and extremely hard to make sense of.

Why is approximate truth hard to make sense of?

Truth, like meaning, seems not to come in degrees: something is true or false.

Through which route does the realist link empirical success to reference?

Success → approximate truth → reference.

Why does Laudan deny that reference and empirical success go together?

Referring theories can be unsuccessful (old tectonic plate theory), and successful theories can fail to refer (aether).

How do aether and phlogiston theories show that success does not indicate approximate truth?

Their central terms do not refer, so they cannot be approximately true, yet they were successful.

What is the first of Saatsi's two readings of the pessimistic meta-induction?

An induction on the past: past successful theories were false, so current ones are likely false too.

What is the second of Saatsi's two readings of the pessimistic meta-induction?

A timeless argument against the no-miracles argument: success is not a reliable indicator of truth.

Why is Saatsi's second reading of the pessimistic meta-induction harder to resist than the first?

It need not predict today’s theories are false. Past successful-but-false theories alone break the success-to-truth inference.
